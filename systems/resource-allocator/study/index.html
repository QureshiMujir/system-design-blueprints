30. Mental Models & First Principles

  1. MM1 — An offer is a revocable lease, not ownership. The scheduler never owns a node; it holds a time-boxed, epoch-stamped claim. Predicts: why offers must expire and why acceptance is racy. Bug without it: treating offers as durable → double-booking. Resolved in: §8 (offer protocol) and Phase 5 (§17).
  2. MM2 — DRF equalizes the dominant share, not each resource. Fairness across resource types means equalizing each tenant's max-normalized resource, not giving equal CPU and equal RAM. Predicts: the §8.5 3-vs-2 split. Bug without it: "fair" schedulers that starve mem-heavy tenants. Resolved in: §7 (I8) and Phase 7 (§19).
  3. MM3 — The WAL is the database; RAM is a cache. Durable truth is the log; in-memory state is a rebuildable projection. Predicts: why nothing is visible before commit (I7) and why recovery is replay. Bug without it: mutating RAM then losing it on crash → divergence. Resolved in: Phase 2 (§14) and §7 (I3/I7).
  4. MM4 — Fence, don't detect. You cannot reliably tell a dead node from a slow one; you can only fence a possibly-live holder by epoch. Predicts: why failover needs epochs before reissue. Bug without it: reissuing a lease a slow node still serves → double-allocation. Resolved in: §7 (I11) and Phase 9 (§21).
  5. MM5 — Two-level scheduling trades global optimality for decentralization. Offering resources to frameworks gives autonomy at the cost of a globally-optimal packing. Predicts: why Mesos scales frameworks but can fragment. Bug without it: expecting monolithic-optimal placement from an offer model. Resolved in: §3 and Phase 4 (§16).
  6. MM6 — Fragmentation is the real capacity ceiling. A cluster at 70% utilization can be unschedulable for large shapes because free capacity is scattered. Predicts: why packing scoring matters more than raw headroom. Bug without it: provisioning by average utilization and still failing placements. Resolved in: §29 and §32.
  7. MM7 — Backpressure is admission control at every hop. A bounded queue that rejects is a hop-local admission controller. Predicts: why every stream must be bounded to avoid OOM cascades. Bug without it: unbounded ingest → memory blow-up under load (FM-9). Resolved in: §6 (P2) and §24.
  8. MM8 — A monotonic epoch linearizes failover. One always-increasing number turns split-brain into a stale-write rejection. Predicts: why an old leader's writes are safely dropped. Bug without it: two leaders both commit → divergence. Resolved in: §7 (I2) and Phase 9 (§21).
  9. MM9 — Exactly-once is at-least-once plus idempotency. You cannot get exactly-once delivery over an unreliable network; you get exactly-once effect by making the receiver dedup a retried, at-least-once message. Predicts: why launch/kill RPCs carry an idempotency key. Bug without it: duplicate task launches after a lost ack. Resolved in: §6 (P11), §7 (I13/I17), Phase 5 (§17).
  10. MM10 — Admission is cheaper than eviction. Rejecting work you cannot fairly run costs nothing; admitting it then killing it wastes the work already done and churns the cluster. Predicts: why quota is enforced at submit, not by reaper. Bug without it: admit-then-kill loops (FM-12). Resolved in: §6 (P7), §7 (I4), Phase 7 (§19).

Required reading (§5)

Books

  • Designing Data-Intensive Applications — Kleppmann (2017). Ch. 5 "Replication", Ch. 9 "Consistency and Consensus". Extract: leader/follower replication, linearizability vs. causal, consensus safety/liveness. Applied in §14 (Phase 2) and I3/I7.
  • Database Internals — Petrov (2019). Part I "Storage Engines" (LSM, WAL), Ch. "Log-Structured Storage", "Recovery". Extract: WAL layout, fsync grouping, snapshot/compaction. Applied in §14 and M4.
  • Site Reliability Engineering — Beyer, Jones, Petoff, Murphy (2016). Ch. "Service Level Objectives", "Handling Overload", "Addressing Cascading Failures". Extract: SLO/error-budget math, load shedding, backpressure. Applied in §24 and §4.
  • Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces — Arpaci-Dusseau (2018). Ch. "Scheduling: Proportional Share" (lottery/stride), "Multi-level Feedback". Extract: proportional-share mechanics that DRF generalises. Applied in §16 and §19.
  • The Linux Programming Interface — Kerrisk (2010). Ch. "Process Creation", "Signals", cgroups/namespaces material. Extract: fork/exec/wait, signal forwarding, resource limits. Applied in §18 (Phase 6).
  • Distributed Systems — Tanenbaum & Van Steen (3rd ed., 2017). Ch. "Fault Tolerance", "Coordination". Extract: failure models, leader election, agreement. Applied in §13 and §21.
  • Systems Performance — Gregg (2nd ed., 2020). Ch. "Methodology" (USE method), "CPU", "Applications". Extract: profiling method, flame graphs. Applied in §28.

Papers

  • Mesos: A Platform for Fine-Grained Resource Sharing in the Data Center — Hindman et al., NSDI 2011. Contribution: two-level scheduling via resource offers. Relevance: the offer lifecycle. Applied in §16, §17, ADR-23.
  • Dominant Resource Fairness: Fair Allocation of Multiple Resource Types — Ghodsi et al., NSDI 2011. Contribution: DRF, sharing incentive, strategy-proofness, Pareto efficiency. Relevance: fairness math. Applied in §19 and I8.
  • Apache Hadoop YARN: Yet Another Resource Negotiator — Vavilapalli et al., SoCC 2013. Contribution: RM/NM/AM decomposition, request-based scheduling. Relevance: centralised scheduling counterpoint. Applied in §3, §16, §34.
  • Large-scale cluster management at Google with Borg — Verma et al., EuroSys 2015. Contribution: priority tiers, admission, quota, allocs, feasibility/scoring. Relevance: production governance. Applied in §19, §24.
  • Omega: flexible, scalable schedulers for large compute clusters — Schwarzkopf et al., EuroSys 2013. Contribution: shared-state optimistic-concurrency scheduling. Relevance: rejected alternative architecture. Applied in §34 and ADR-23.
  • Multi-Resource Packing for Cluster Schedulers (Tetris) — Grandl et al., SIGCOMM 2014. Contribution: packing as multi-dimensional bin-packing, alignment scoring. Relevance: placement scoring. Applied in §16 and §29.
  • Quasar: Resource-Efficient and QoS-Aware Cluster Management — Delimitrou & Kozyrakis, ASPLOS 2014. Contribution: classification-driven right-sizing. Relevance: deferred right-sizing. Applied in §34 and §35.
  • In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm (Raft) — Ongaro & Ousterhout, USENIX ATC 2014. Contribution: leader election, log replication, membership change. Relevance: metadata store. Applied in §14 and ADR-09.
  • The Tail at Scale — Dean & Barroso, CACM 2013. Contribution: tail-latency amplification, hedged requests. Relevance: scheduler latency budget. Applied in §28 and §4.
  • Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems — Chandra & Toueg, JACM 1996. Contribution: ◇P failure-detector properties. Relevance: heartbeat design. Applied in §13 and I5.
  • Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System — Lamport, CACM 1978. Contribution: happens-before, logical clocks. Relevance: event ordering. Applied in §8 and ADR-20.
  • Logical Physical Clocks and Consistent Snapshots in Globally Distributed Databases (HLC) — Kulkarni, Demirbas et al., OPODIS 2014. Contribution: hybrid logical clocks. Relevance: skew-tolerant ordering. Applied in ADR-20 and §8.
  • Quincy: Fair Scheduling for Distributed Computing Clusters — Isard et al., SOSP 2009. Contribution: scheduling as a min-cost flow trading fairness vs. locality. Relevance: an alternative fairness formulation. Applied in §19.
  • Sparrow: Distributed, Low Latency Scheduling — Ousterhout et al., SOSP 2013. Contribution: decentralized sampling-based scheduling. Relevance: the gossip/decentralized alternative. Applied in §34 (Alternative C).

Reference Implementations

  • apache/mesos — src/master/, src/slave/, src/master/allocator/mesos/hierarchical.cpp. Lesson: offer lifecycle, allocator interface. Pitfall not to copy: offer starvation when one framework hoards offers. Applied in §16, §17.
  • apache/hadoop (hadoop-yarn-server-resourcemanager: scheduler/capacity/, scheduler/fair/). Lesson: hierarchical capacity/fair queues, preemption monitor. Pitfall: heartbeat-driven scheduling adds a heartbeat of latency per allocation. Applied in §19.
  • kubernetes/kubernetes — pkg/scheduler/ (framework/, framework/plugins/noderesources/). Lesson: pluggable filter/score extension points. Pitfall: a single monolithic scheduler goroutine caps throughput. Applied in §16, §34.
  • hashicorp/nomad — scheduler/ (generic_sched.go, feasible.go, rank.go), nomad/plan_apply.go. Lesson: optimistic placement + serialised plan-apply. Pitfall: baked-in single-region assumptions. Applied in §16, §17.
  • etcd-io/etcd — server/storage/wal/, raft/. Lesson: WAL segment format, snapshot cadence. Pitfall: full-keyspace watch fan-out does not scale to our heartbeat rate. Applied in §14.
  • cockroachdb/cockroach — pkg/kv/kvserver/ (Raft), pkg/storage/ (Pebble). Lesson: Raft-over-LSM integration, snapshot streaming. Pitfall: don't couple the state machine to a SQL layer. Applied in §14.

Glossary (§36) · 60 terms · site-wide glossary

Agent
per-node daemon that probes capacity, heartbeats, and supervises executors.
Admission control
accept/reject decision at submit based on quota headroom (I4, P7).
Allocation
capacity assigned to a workload, made durable as a lease (I1).
Anti-affinity
constraint forbidding co-placement of two workloads (§8.4).
Attempt
a numbered retry of a workload; key for idempotent launch (I13).
Available capacity
total − allocated − reserved, per node/resource (I10).
Backpressure
bounded queues + credit flow-control shedding load at each hop (P2, ADR-21).
Borrowing
a queue temporarily using idle capacity above its guarantee, reclaimable (I4).
Capacity conservation
allocated + reserved + available = total, always (I10, P9).
Control plane
the manager cluster making scheduling/allocation decisions.
DRF (Dominant Resource Fairness)
fairness equalizing each tenant's dominant (max-normalized) share (I8, MM2).
Dominant resource
the resource type a tenant uses the largest normalized fraction of.
Epoch
monotonically increasing cluster generation; fences stale writers (I2, MM8).
Executor
the process that launches and supervises a workload under isolation.
Failure detector
heartbeat-age logic classifying nodes SUSPECT/LOST (I5).
Fencing
invalidating a possibly-live holder by epoch before reissuing its lease (I11, P4, MM4).
Framework
an external scheduler that accepts offers (Mesos model, ADR-23).
Fragmentation
scattered free capacity that can't fit large shapes (MM6).
Gang scheduling
all-or-nothing placement of a set of tasks (I9).
Guaranteed share
the capacity a queue is entitled to before borrowing.
Headroom
reserved slack to absorb bursts (Borg-style).
Heartbeat
periodic agent liveness + capacity report (interval 2 s).
Hybrid Logical Clock (HLC)
skew-tolerant causal timestamp (ADR-20).
Lease
a durable, time-boxed, single-owner allocation record (I1, I7).
Manager
a control-plane replica; one is Raft leader.
Offer
a revocable, epoch-stamped resource proposal to a framework (P8, MM1).
Overcommit ratio
factor by which advertised capacity exceeds physical.
Preemption
reclaiming resources from lower-priority work for higher (§19, I14).
Placement scoring
ranking feasible nodes (Tetris alignment + soft constraints, §16).
Quorum
majority of managers required to commit (Raft).
Quota
per-tenant guaranteed/max share enforced at admission (I4).
Raft
the consensus protocol backing the WAL (ADR-09).
Reconciliation
converging agent reality to manager intent (§15).
Reserved capacity
capacity held out of scheduling (static reservations/headroom).
RPO / RTO
recovery point / time objectives (0 / ≤ 300 s, §4).
Snapshot
a consistent state image at one committed log index (I12).
Split-brain
two managers both believing they lead; prevented by I2 (MM8).
State machine
the deterministic RAM projection of the WAL (P1, MM3).
Two-level scheduling
resources offered to frameworks that decide (MM5, ADR-23).
WAL (write-ahead log)
the durable, ordered record of all mutations (P3, I3).
Workload
a submitted unit of work with a resource request and lifecycle (§8.2).
Accrual failure detector
a detector emitting a graded suspicion level (φ) rather than a boolean (§13).
Affinity
a constraint preferring or requiring co-placement (§8.4).
Backfill
placing smaller work into holes a blocked large workload leaves (§16).
Bin-packing
fitting multi-dimensional resource shapes onto nodes to reduce fragmentation (Tetris, §16).
Copy-on-write view
an immutable per-epoch inventory snapshot read lock-free by scheduler shards (§15, P5).
Credit flow-control
receiver-granted credits bounding in-flight data per stream (ADR-21).
Delay scheduling
briefly waiting for a preferred (local) node before placing elsewhere (§16).
Drain
stopping new placement on a node and migrating its work on exit (§15, §24).
Feature-epoch gating
proposing a new WAL entry type only once every quorum member supports it (§25).
Feasibility filter
the hard-constraint stage that shrinks candidate nodes (§16).
Group commit
batching WAL appends into one fsync to amortise latency (§14, M4).
Hierarchical queue
a tree of queues with per-node guaranteed/max shares (§19).
Idempotency key
the (workload, attempt, epoch) tuple making launches retry-safe (I13).
Joint consensus
Raft's two-phase membership change avoiding split quorums (§14).
Pre-vote
a Raft round preventing disruptive term inflation on rejoin (§21).
Revocable resource
capacity lent out that can be reclaimed (over-subscription, §35).
SPIFFE SVID
a short-lived cryptographic workload identity used for mTLS (ADR-18).
Suspicion level
the accrual detector's continuous liveness estimate (§13).
Taint / toleration
a node repellent and the workload marker that overrides it (§8.4).
