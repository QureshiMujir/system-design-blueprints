33. Mental Models & First Principles

  • MM1 — A timer wheel is a radix sort of deadlines, computed incrementally. The hierarchical level assignment (level = ⌊log₆₄(Δticks)⌋) is a base-64 radix decomposition of the offset; cascading is that decomposition's carry-propagate step, computed one carry at a time instead of all at once. Predicts: insert is O(1) not because deadlines go unsorted, but because the "sort" is deferred until the exact tick that needs the answer. Bug class without it: treating cascade as a one-time bucket move instead of a re-sort against the current now (§16.6's stale-Δticks pitfall). Applied at §16, §8.5.
  • MM2 — Two clocks, one truth: monotonic is "when," wall is "what it means to others." Every production clock bug conflates "duration since a fixed point" with "a value everyone agrees on." Predicts: any function whose signature doesn't statically distinguish the two will eventually be called with the wrong one under skew. Bug class without it: encoding both as bare u64 nanoseconds and trusting code review — exactly what P1's newtypes make a compile error instead. Applied at §13, §6 P1.
  • MM3 — Durability is a property of the log, not of the data structure. The wheel/ heap in memory are disposable; the WAL is what survives a crash, and recovery re-derives the wheel from the log, never the reverse. Predicts: any optimization skipping the WAL append to make the wheel "faster" silently breaks crash safety regardless of the wheel's in-memory correctness. Bug class without it: "the wheel already knows the deadline, why log it again" reasoning. Applied at §22, P10.
  • MM4 — Bounding by wall-time, not work-count, is how a background sweep stays safe inside a real-time budget. Any "sweep in the background" system (GC, compaction, active-expire) bounded by count instead of time inherits an unbounded worst case from its input distribution. Predicts: a skewed workload eventually violates any count-based bound, however conservative. Bug class without it: "sample at most 20 per cycle" reasoning that assumes uniform occupancy, breaking the instant one shard is hot. Applied at §18, P5, I10.
  • MM5 — Idempotent replay turns "exactly once" into "at least once" for free. Once every mutation carries a monotonic idempotency key (Generation), at-least-once redelivery converges to the same state without distributed exactly-once machinery. Predicts: any generation/version-checked apply path tolerates duplicate delivery from crash recovery or replication with no extra dedup table. Bug class without it: bolting on ad-hoc "have I seen this message ID" tables instead of making the state transition naturally idempotent. Applied at §7 I2/I7/I12, P12, §22, §23.
  • MM6 — A hybrid logical clock is a Lamport clock wearing a wall-clock costume. HLC's wall_ms gives callers the wall-time meaning they actually want (EXPIREAT semantics) while its logical component gives strict causal ordering a pure wall clock cannot guarantee for concurrent same-millisecond events. Predicts: any distributed system needing both "roughly real time" and "definitely ordered" converges on this exact construction, independent of domain. Bug class without it: pure wall-clock ordering silently reorders concurrent writes at the same timestamp; pure logical clocks lose the wall-time meaning entirely. Applied at §23, §8.4.
  • MM7 — Fixed-capacity overflow-to-a-bigger-structure beats growing the fast structure. The heap-top's fixed-256-with-eviction-to-wheel design keeps the hot/small path bounded and simple, spilling overflow into the already-general slow path. Predicts: any "hot cache in front of a general store" design benefits from this same fixed-size-with- spillover shape. Bug class without it: an elastic heap-top reintroducing the O(log n)- at-scale cost the wheel exists to avoid. Applied at §17, ADR-04, §35 D4.
  • MM8 — Sharding trades global correctness reasoning for bounded blast radius. A single global lock is easy to reason about but has unbounded blast radius; N shards bound the blast radius to 1/N at the cost of N times as many invariants to hold independently. Predicts: any "hot-key isolation" claim is only as good as its shard-count-to-worst-case-skew ratio. Bug class without it: assuming sharding alone eliminates contention when one sufficiently hot key still saturates its one shard. Applied at §6 P11, §18.7, §32.

5. Required Reading

Books

  • Arpaci-Dusseau, R. & A. — Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces, "Scheduling" and "I/O" chapters. Foundational cron-driver and interrupt-vs-poll reasoning — applied §13, §19.
  • Kleppmann, M. — Designing Data-Intensive Applications, ch. 5 (Replication), ch. 9 (Consistency & Consensus). Replication topology and clock-skew framing — applied §23.
  • Petrov, A. — Database Internals, WAL and recovery chapters. Log record layout, checkpointing, recovery discipline — applied §22.
  • Bernstein, P. & Newcomer, E. — Principles of Transaction Processing, log-management chapters. Group-commit and durability-vs-latency tradeoffs — applied §22.
  • Herlihy, M. & Shavit, N. — The Art of Multiprocessor Programming, lock-free/ concurrent structure chapters. Per-shard concurrency discipline, ABA-style hazards in the arena-backed wheel — applied §14, §16, §19.
  • Gregg, B. — Systems Performance, latency/tail-latency methodology chapters. Benchmark and profiling methodology — applied §24, §31.

Papers

  • Varghese, G. & Lauck, T. (1987). Hashed and Hierarchical Timing Wheels: Efficient Data Structures for Implementing a Timer Facility. IEEE/ACM ToN. The foundational algorithm and cost model — applied §14, §16.
  • Lamport, L. (1978). Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System. CACM. Logical ordering primitive HLC builds on — applied §23.
  • Kulkarni, S., Demirbas, M., Madappa, D., Avva, B., Leone, M. (2014). Logical Physical Clocks. OPODIS. The HLC construction used verbatim for tombstone ordering — applied §23.
  • Mohan, C., Haderle, D., Lindsay, B., Pirahesh, H., Schwarz, P. (1992). ARIES: A Transaction Recovery Method Supporting Fine-Granularity Locking and Partial Rollbacks Using Write-Ahead Logging. ACM TODS. WAL/recovery discipline this engine's group-commit and replay algorithm derives from — applied §22.
  • Helland, P. (2007). Life Beyond Distributed Transactions: an Apostate's Opinion. CIDR. Idempotent-replay and at-least-once framing — applied §23, §26.
  • Cao, P., Felten, E., Li, K. (1994). Implementation and Performance of Application-Controlled File Caching. USENIX OSDI. The eviction-vs-expiration boundary this engine deliberately does not cross — applied §3, §18.
  • Cohen, E. & Kaplan, H. (2002). Aging Through Cascaded Caches: Performance Issues in the Distribution of Web Content. SIGCOMM. Cascaded-structure aging cost model informing the wheel's cascade-cost reasoning — applied §16, §18.
  • Mills, D. (2003). NTP Architecture, Protocol, and Algorithms. Internet-Draft / RFC lineage. Skew-tolerance modeling for wall_to_monotonic and replicated fire accuracy — applied §13, §23.
  • Fitzpatrick, B. (2004). Distributed Caching with Memcached. Linux Journal. Lazy on-access expiration precedent — applied §18.

Reference Implementations

  • Redis — src/expire.c (activeExpireCycle), src/db.c (lookupKeyReadWithFlags). Lesson: bound the active cycle by wall time and adapt sample size to observed hit ratio (ADR-06). Pitfall: tightly coupled to the global keyspace dict — not reusable standalone; this engine extracts the algorithm, not the coupling — applied §18.2.
  • Apache Kafka — core/src/main/scala/kafka/utils/timer/TimingWheel.scala, SystemTimer.scala. Lesson: hierarchical wheel married to an unbounded DelayQueue overflow bucket instead of a fixed-capacity heap-top. Pitfall: unbounded overflow bucket risks GC pressure under a skewed TTL distribution — this engine's fixed-256 heap-top with eviction-to-wheel is the deliberate alternative — applied (contrast) §16, §17.2.
  • Netty — io.netty.util.HashedWheelTimer. Lesson: single background worker thread, a "rounds" counter lets a single-level wheel represent deadlines beyond one rotation. Pitfall: an unbounded per-tick task queue can starve the worker thread — this engine's arena has a hard capacity — applied (contrast) §14, §16.6.
  • Linux kernel — kernel/time/timer.c (classic cascading wheel), kernel/time/ hrtimer.c (per-CPU red-black tree for high-resolution timers). Lesson: the kernel picks wheel-vs-tree per accuracy class rather than hybridizing one structure. Pitfall: migrating timers across CPUs on hotplug is a documented kernel wart this engine avoids by not being CPU-pinned — applied (contrast) §16, §17.
  • Go runtime — runtime/time.go (per-P four-ary heap, netpoll integration). Lesson: a per-P heap avoids a global lock entirely. Pitfall: per-P heaps complicate a single global horizon view — this engine's per-shard KeyState map plus one global wheel is the deliberate simpler alternative — applied (contrast) §15, §19, §16.6.
  • Tokio — tokio/src/runtime/time/wheel/. Lesson: the exact 6-level × 64-slot hierarchical-wheel-plus-heap-top hybrid this engine's Phase 5 is structurally identical to. Pitfall: driver-thread starvation under heavy async task scheduling — mitigated here by the cron driver being a dedicated OS thread, not a cooperative task — applied §17, §19.7.

RFCs / Specifications

  • RFC 5905 — Network Time Protocol Version 4. Skew bound and step-vs-slew semantics — applied §13 (wall_to_monotonic), §23 (replicated fire-accuracy tolerance).
  • Redis Serialization Protocol (RESP2/RESP3), redis.io/docs/reference/protocol-spec. Wire format the frontend implements byte-for-byte — applied §21.
  • Redis command reference — EXPIRE/PEXPIRE/EXPIREAT/PEXPIREAT/TTL/PTTL/ PERSIST. Return-value and flag (NX/XX/GT/LT) semantics mirrored exactly — applied §21.

Glossary (§39) · 45 terms · site-wide glossary

Active-expire cycle
the wall-time-bounded background sweep that fires already-past deadlines the wheel/heap-top have not yet reached (§18.2, ADR-06).
ARIES
Algorithm for Recovery and Isolation Exploiting Semantics; the WAL/recovery discipline this engine's group-commit and replay derive from (Mohan et al. 1992, §5).
Arena
a pre-allocated pool of fixed-size node slots the wheel/heap draw from, eliminating per-fire heap allocation (§8.2, I11).
Blast radius
the scope of a system affected by a single failure or hot key; bounded to one shard by P11.
Cascade
the process of draining a wheel level's current slot and re-inserting its entries into a lower level on cursor wrap (§16.2, §8.5).
Clock domain
the distinction between monotonic time (scheduling) and wall time (meaning to other processes); enforced by newtypes (P1, §13).
Conformance suite
the automated test harness comparing actual behavior against named fixtures across every phase (§29, per-phase §N.5 tables).
Cron driver
the single dedicated thread advancing the wheel/heap-top and running the active-expire cycle once per tick (§19.1).
CRC32C
the Castagnoli cyclic redundancy check polynomial used as the WAL record integrity trailer, hardware-accelerated on x86-64 (§22.2).
DashMap
the lock-striped concurrent hash map used for the per-shard KeyId → KeyState map (ADR-16).
Deadline
a (key, generation, MonotonicNs) tuple representing a scheduled fire event (§8.2, §11).
DurableSink
a notification sink that WAL-logs before delivery, giving exactly-once-modulo-ack semantics at the cost of two fsyncs per fire (§20.2, ADR-19).
Fire
the moment a deadline's target time is reached and its notification is enqueued (§8.6 state-transition table).
Fire jitter
the observed delay between a deadline's target time and its actual fire time (§4, M6/M7).
Generation
a per-key monotonic counter that lets stale wheel entries be silently discarded rather than requiring active removal (§8.2, I7, P12).
Group commit
batching multiple WAL appends into one fsync, bounded by time or record count, whichever is sooner (ADR-09, §22.2).
Handle
an opaque (key, generation) pair returned to callers for later cancel/CAS operations (§11).
Heap-top
the fixed-capacity (256) sorted-heap structure holding near-term deadlines for sub-tick fire accuracy (§17, ADR-04).
Hierarchical timing wheel
the multi-level cascading wheel structure from Varghese & Lauck 1987 that gives O(1)-amortised insert at unbounded horizon (§16).
HLC (Hybrid Logical Clock)
a (wall_ms, logical, node_id) timestamp combining wall- time meaning with strict causal ordering, used for replicated tombstone/insert ordering (§8.4, §23.2, Kulkarni et al. 2014).
Horizon
the maximum representable deadline offset a wheel can schedule without rejection (§8.1, §35 D1).
Idempotent replay
the property that applying a mutation once or twice yields identical state, enabled by the Generation field (P12, MM5).
Invariant
a statement that must hold at all times, enforced and telemetered per §7's table (I1–I13).
Keyspace event
a notification emitted on key expiration, compatible with Redis's keyspace-notification pub/sub format (§20.2).
KeyState
the per-shard map value holding a key's current generation and deadline (§11).
Lazy expiration
checking and firing a deadline on key access rather than waiting for the active cycle to reach it (§18.2, ADR-07).
Level (wheel)
one of the six tiers of the hierarchical wheel, each advancing 64× slower than the level below it (§8.1).
MAX_SINK_DELIVER_DURATION
the 100ms default timeout bounding any single Sink::deliver call (§11, I13).
MonotonicNs
the newtype wrapping CLOCK_MONOTONIC-sourced nanoseconds; the engine's internal scheduling time domain (§11, P1).
NTP skew
the divergence between a host's wall clock and true time, bounded by a configured tolerance for replicated fire-accuracy purposes (§4, M13).
Ns
a signed nanosecond duration type, the arithmetic result of subtracting two MonotonicNs/WallNs values (§11).
PastHorizon
the typed error returned when an insert's deadline exceeds the wheel's maximum representable offset (P8, §8.7).
Persist (PERSIST)
the operation that cancels a key's TTL by incrementing its generation and freeing its KeyState (§8.6, §11).
RESP (Redis Serialization Protocol)
the wire protocol this engine's frontend implements byte-compatibly (§5, §21).
Shard
one of 256 fixed partitions of the KeyId → KeyState map, bounding lock contention blast radius (P11, §6).
Sink
the pluggable trait for delivering fire notifications to a consumer (keyspace, webhook, durable, or custom) (§11, §20).
Snapshot
a point-in-time serialization of shard-map and wheel state, pinning WAL replay to a known LSN and bounding recovery RTO (§22.2, ADR-10, §35 D6).
Split-brain
a failure mode where two nodes both believe themselves the write leader during a partition; explicitly out of scope for single-leader replication (§23.8, §26).
Stale entry (wheel)
a wheel node whose generation no longer matches its key's current KeyState, silently dropped on fire (§8.6, I7).
Tick
the fixed 1ms (default) unit of wheel/cron-driver advancement (§8.1, ADR-05).
Tombstone
a WAL record marking a fired or persisted deadline as resolved, used in recovery and replication ordering (§8.3, §22.2).
Top horizon
the offset threshold below which a deadline routes to the heap-top instead of the wheel (default 16ms) (§17.2, ADR-04).
WAL (Write-Ahead Log)
the append-only durability log that is the sole source of truth for outstanding deadlines across a crash (§22, I3, I8, P10).
WallNs
the newtype wrapping CLOCK_REALTIME-sourced nanoseconds since the Unix epoch; used only at the API boundary and for replicated absolute deadlines (§11, P1).
Wheel epoch
the fixed reference MonotonicNs origin against which a wheel's slot indices are computed (§11, §12.6).
