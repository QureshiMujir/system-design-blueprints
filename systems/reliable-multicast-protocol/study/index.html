33. Hard Questions (Self-Examination)

  • Q1. Why does persistence-before-eligibility (P13, §18) not simply become persistence-before-delivery, and what would break in the p50 publish-latency budget (§4) if it did?
  • Q2. How does the 512 MB reorder-buffer ceiling (§4, §15) interact with the 50,000,000-packet replay backlog ceiling (§4, §18) when a single receiver falls far enough behind to need replay instead of live reorder?
  • Q3. If the timer wheel (§16) and the congestion controller's pacing epochs (§19) both want to control retry timing, which one actually owns a retry's schedule, and how is that resolved without violating I6's tick-monotonicity?
  • Q4. Why is membership (§20) explicitly non-consensus while sequencing (§13, §15) is strictly single-authority — what would happen to Invariant I1 if membership agreement were required before a sequence number could be allocated?
  • Q5. Given the 1.5x amplification ceiling (§4, I7) and the 8% burst-loss tolerance (§4), what repair-suppression window (§17, §32 C9) is actually implied, and does it hold at the 50,000-receiver membership target (§20)?
  • Q6. Why does the WAL's checksum-and-quarantine design (I8, §18) not by itself satisfy the availability target (§4, §32 C11) — what additional mechanism from §20 (membership) is implicitly required to hit RTO after a quarantine event?
  • Q7. If clock skew (P12, §8) can never be trusted for correctness, how does the congestion controller (§19) measure RTT at all, and what invariant prevents a skewed RTT estimate from corrupting the pacing epoch (I11)?
  • Q8. Why does Design Principle P9 (congestion safety over throughput) not conflict with the 5 Gbps sustained-throughput target (§4), and what capacity-model number (§32) resolves the apparent tension?
  • Q9. What breaks first under sustained 8% burst loss (§4) — the reorder-buffer ceiling (§15), the amplification ceiling (§17, I7), or the WAL write-amplification budget (§18) — and in what order do the DEGRADED-state transitions (§8) fire?
  • Q10. Why is Forward Error Correction deferred (§35, anchor §17) rather than built in Phase 5, given that NORM/RFC 5740 (§5) offers it as an option — what would FEC change about the amplification-ceiling derivation in §32 C5?
  • Q11. How does rejecting kernel-bypass transport (§1, §34 Alt A) interact with the per-core throughput budget derived in §32 C12 — does the syscall-batching approach in §14 actually close that gap?
  • Q12. Why does the Sequencing Engine's single-authority model (§13, I1) not become a bottleneck at the 5 Gbps throughput target (§4), and what would have to change in §11's PacketSequence design if it did?
  • Q13. If observability must never become a second failure domain (MM8, §21), how does the failure-snapshot dump mechanism (§21) avoid violating the bounded-memory principle (P2, §6) during exactly the incident it is meant to capture?
  • Q14. Why does the suppression-cache sharding fix in §17 (shard by (stream_id, window), not by receiver) not reintroduce the same hot-shard problem the timer wheel's bucket-load-aware assignment (§16) was designed to avoid?
  • Q15. What is the actual relationship between the RPO (§4) and the fsync-batching interval chosen in §18 — could a batching interval that satisfies the WAL write-amplification budget (§4, §32 C6) ever violate RPO, and under what load profile?
  • Q16. Given membership convergence must complete in < 10 s (§4, §20) and heartbeat processing has a derived floor of 5,000 receivers/s (§32 C10), what happens to convergence time if the receiver count approaches the FD-budget-implied ceiling of ~1,365 concurrent streams (§32 C4) rather than the 200-stream horizontal target?
  • Q17. Why does Alt C's virtual-synchrony rejection (§34) not contradict the decision to study Corosync's Totem protocol as a reference implementation (§5) — what specifically was salvaged versus rejected?
  • Q18. If a receiver's clock is skewed enough to misjudge heartbeat staleness (FM-9, §23), what stops the same skew from also corrupting its interpretation of the diagnostic-only packet timestamp (§8) in a way that misleads an operator during postmortem (§21) without ever violating a correctness invariant?

30. Mental Models & First Principles

  • MM1 — Suppression Is a Rate Limiter, Not a Correctness Mechanism. Suppression windows bound repair-traffic rate; they do not by themselves guarantee eventual delivery. Predicts: sustained loss still needs a retry/timeout escalation path beyond suppression alone. Bug class without it: assuming "suppressed" means "handled" and omitting the escalation path, causing silent permanent gaps. Applies in §17.
  • MM2 — Bounded Memory Beats Optimal Memory. A hard ceiling with predictable degradation (P2) beats an unbounded structure that is usually smaller but occasionally OOMs. Predicts: burst loss eventually hits the ceiling, so eviction policy is never optional. Bug class without it: sizing structures for the common case and getting paged on the tail case. Applies in §15.
  • MM3 — No Per-Receiver ACK Means Aggregate Signals Only. Multicast congestion control cannot see individual receiver health directly, only aggregate NACK volume. Predicts: any per-receiver fairness mechanism must be inferred, never directly measured. Bug class without it: designing the controller as if it had a TCP-style per-flow ACK clock. Applies in §19.
  • MM4 — Persistence-Before-Delivery Inverts the Usual Latency Tradeoff. Durability gates replay-eligibility, not live delivery (P13); live delivery is faster than durable-replay-eligible. Predicts: "delivered" and "replayable" are different guarantees with different latencies. Bug class without it: assuming delivered implies replayable and being surprised by a post-crash recovery gap. Applies in §18.
  • MM5 — A Timer Facility's Scaling Bound Is Insert/Expire Cost, Not Storage. At millions of timers, per-operation complexity (O(1) wheel vs. O(log n) heap) dominates, not raw memory. Predicts: a heap-based timer looks fine in a unit test and fails only at production cardinality. Bug class without it: benchmarking a timer facility at the wrong scale and shipping a heap. Applies in §16.
  • MM6 — Membership Is Advisory, Not a Quorum. This system's membership view optimizes repair targeting and telemetry; it is never a correctness dependency. Predicts: a split-brain membership view degrades efficiency, not correctness. Bug class without it: building delivery-ordering logic that depends on membership agreement, silently reintroducing the consensus dependency the architecture explicitly avoided. Applies in §20.
  • MM7 — Sequence Numbers Are the Only Trustworthy Clock. Wall-clock and cross-node monotonic-clock readings cannot be trusted for ordering (P12); only the single-authority sequence number can. Predicts: any ordering logic keyed on timestamp instead of sequence misorders under clock skew. Bug class without it: a "sort by timestamp" shortcut that works with synced dev clocks and misorders in production. Applies in §15.
  • MM8 — Observability Must Never Become a Second Failure Domain. Telemetry paths are queue-fed and backpressured (P8) specifically so a telemetry outage cannot cause a packet-path outage. Predicts: any synchronous telemetry call on the hot path reintroduces exactly the coupling this principle forbids. Bug class without it: a "temporary" synchronous metrics-export call that becomes a production outage cause. Applies in §21.

5. Required Reading

Books

  • TCP/IP Illustrated, Volume 1 — W. Richard Stevens (1994), ch. on IP multicasting and IGMP: multicast group-join and TTL/scoping fundamentals; applied in §14 and §20.
  • Designing Data-Intensive Applications — Martin Kleppmann (2017), ch. 5 (Replication): replay/log-shipping semantics and the durability-before-ack pattern; applied in §18 and §6.
  • Systems Performance — Brendan Gregg (2020, 2nd ed.), ch. on networking and observability: syscall-batching diagnosis and tail-latency methodology; applied in §28.
  • Understanding Linux Network Internals — Christian Benvenuti (2005), ch. on the socket layer and IGMP: socket-buffer sizing and multicast group membership internals; applied in §14.
  • The Linux Programming Interface — Michael Kerrisk (2010), ch. on epoll and sockets: nonblocking IO polling model for the transport worker loop; applied in §14 and §12.
  • Computer Networks — Andrew S. Tanenbaum (5th ed., 2010), ch. on multicast routing: protocol-modeling context for IGMP/PIM assumptions; applied in §22.
  • Distributed Systems: Principles and Paradigms — Tanenbaum & Van Steen (3rd ed., 2017), ch. on fault tolerance: crash-recovery semantics informing §23's recovery classification; applied in §23.
  • High Performance Browser Networking — Ilya Grigorik (2013), ch. on TCP congestion control: pacing intuition adapted for sender-driven multicast pacing; applied in §19.

Papers

  • "A Reliable Multicast Framework for Light-weight Sessions and Application Level Framing" — Floyd, Jacobson, Liu, McCanne & Zhang, 1997, IEEE/ACM ToN (SRM): NACK-based recovery and receiver-driven suppression → basis for the repair-suppression design; applied in §17.
  • "RMTP: A Reliable Multicast Transport Protocol" — Lin & Paul, 1996, IEEE INFOCOM: hierarchical ACK aggregation for scalable multicast recovery → informs the receiver-tree tradeoff rejected in §34; applied in §34.
  • "BBR: Congestion-Based Congestion Control" — Cardwell, Cheng, Gunn, Yeganeh & Jacobson, 2016/2017, ACM Queue / CACM: model-based pacing over loss-reactive AIMD → basis for ADR-23's pacing strategy; applied in §19.
  • "Chain Replication for Supporting High Throughput and Availability" — van Renesse & Schneider, 2004, OSDI: ordered replay and durability-before-visibility → informs the WAL-then-deliver ordering in §18; applied in §18 and §15.
  • "Dapper, a Large-Scale Distributed Systems Tracing Infrastructure" — Sigelman et al., 2010, Google Technical Report: causal span-chain tracing at fan-out scale → basis for the packet lifecycle span design; applied in §21.
  • "Hashed and Hierarchical Timing Wheels" — Varghese & Lauck, 1987, SOSP: O(1) timer insert/expire at high timer cardinality → direct basis for ADR-24 and the Phase 4 timer subsystem; applied in §16.
  • "Congestion Avoidance and Control" — Van Jacobson, 1988, SIGCOMM: additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease pacing discipline → the loss-reactive baseline BBR-inspired pacing is compared against; applied in §19 and §31.
  • The Datacenter as a Computer — Barroso, Clidaras & Hölzle, 2013 (2nd ed.), Synthesis Lectures on Computer Architecture: operational realities of network fabric at datacenter scale → informs the network-fabric trust-boundary assumptions; applied in §22.

Reference Implementations

  • real-logic/aeron — study aeron-driver/src/main/c/media: term-buffer and media-driver architecture for low-latency framing; pitfall: do not copy its shared-memory IPC transport, this system targets UDP-only fan-out; applied in §14.
  • corosync/corosync — study exec/totemsrp.c: Totem single-ring ordered-delivery token protocol; pitfall: do not adopt token-passing membership, it does not scale past small cluster sizes at the 50,000-receiver target; applied in §20.
  • apache/kafka — study core/src/main/scala/kafka/log: append-only log segmentation, rotation, and retention-by-offset; applied in §18.
  • nats-io/nats-server — study server/filestore.go: JetStream file-backed stream replay and segment compaction; applied in §18.
  • spread-toolkit/spread — study daemon/membership.c: lightweight group-membership state machine for unreliable-network multicast; applied in §20.
  • nanomsg/nng — study src/protocol: compact tagged-frame protocol framing without a full RPC layer; applied in §13.
  • cloudflare/quiche — study src/recovery: loss-detection and pacing-scheduler interaction in a real production transport; pitfall: do not copy its per-connection ACK-driven congestion signal, this system has no per-receiver ACK stream; applied in §19.

RFCs / Specifications

  • RFC 1112 — Host Extensions for IP Multicasting: the IGMP group-membership model this system's group-join logic targets; applied in §14.
  • RFC 3376 — IGMPv3: source-filtered multicast membership semantics; applied in §14.
  • RFC 3810 — MLDv2: IPv6 multicast listener discovery, the IPv6 analog of IGMPv3; applied in §14.
  • RFC 3208 — PGM: NACK-based transport semantics and option encoding this wire protocol's REPAIR/NACK types are modeled on; applied in §13.
  • RFC 5740 — NORM: negative-acknowledgment reliability and optional FEC framing, the source of the deferred-FEC design point; applied in §17.
  • RFC 8085 — UDP Usage Guidelines: congestion-safety and rate-limiting guidance for UDP-based protocols; applied in §19.
  • RFC 9000 §17 — QUIC packet/frame framing model: pins down the versioned, extensible frame-type encoding this project's deferred QUIC-fallback transport would adopt; applied in §14.

Glossary (§36) · 46 terms · site-wide glossary

Replay Window
the persistent, recoverable packet range currently servable from WAL segments.
Delivery Cursor
per-receiver progress marker tracking contiguous and observed sequence high-water marks.
Repair Storm
a retransmission-amplification spike caused by synchronized or excessive receiver NACKs.
NACK Suppression
deduplication of redundant repair requests for the same packet window.
Session Epoch
a monotonic generation identifier scoping a stream's sequence space.
WAL (Write-Ahead Log)
the append-only durable store backing replay and crash recovery.
Gap Detection
identification of a missing sequence range at a receiver's delivery cursor.
Packet Amplification
the ratio of retransmitted bytes to primary-sent bytes.
Congestion Epoch
one pacing-adjustment interval in the congestion controller.
Timer Wheel
a hierarchical, bucketed timer structure for O(1) amortized schedule/expire.
Stream Retention
the durability window a stream's WAL segments are guaranteed to cover.
Ordered Delivery
monotonic, in-order application-visible sequencing on a strict-order stream.
Reorder Buffer
bounded, per-receiver temporary storage for out-of-order arrivals.
Multicast Group
the shared IP multicast address/scope a stream's packets are addressed to.
Receiver Quarantine
isolation of a pathological receiver's NACK traffic from shared suppression state.
Replay Snapshot
a persisted, version-tagged recovery checkpoint (SNAPSHOT packet type).
Packet Pacing
controlled, rate-limited transmission scheduling driven by the congestion budget.
Segment Rotation
the WAL event sealing one segment and opening the next.
Heartbeat Interval
the configured liveness-emission period for a receiver's membership state.
Sequence Gap
a missing range in an otherwise monotonic sequence stream.
Suppression Window
the interval during which only one repair request per packet window is honored.
Membership View
the Membership Agent's per-receiver liveness and health record.
Health Score
a blended signal of heartbeat recency and delivery-cursor lag driving degraded-state transitions.
Split Brain
two membership shards disagreeing on the current epoch after a partition.
Crash-Only Recovery
the design discipline of recovering exclusively via cold restart, never in-memory healing.
Backpressure
explicit, visible publisher throttling applied before runtime queue exhaustion.
Cardinality Guard
the enforcement point rejecting telemetry label combinations past a configured bound.
Token Bucket
the rate-shaping structure enforcing a congestion epoch's allowed send rate.
Fsync Batching
grouping multiple WAL appends into one durable-sync call to amortize latency.
Sparse Index
the mmap-backed, non-exhaustive replay index mapping sequence ranges to segment offsets.
Degraded State
the receiver lifecycle state entered under repair saturation or slow-consumer backlog.
Delivery Barrier
the mechanism preventing out-of-order application-visible delivery on strict-order streams.
Dedup Table
the structure enforcing duplicate-suppression at the delivery boundary (I4).
Congestion Budget
the per-epoch computed allowed-send-rate, repair-rate, and burst-budget bundle.
Burst Budget
the instantaneous-spend allowance within a congestion epoch's paced rate.
Amplification Ratio
the live metric tracking retransmitted-to-primary byte ratio against the I7 ceiling.
Suppression Collision
a detected double-entry attempt in the repair suppression cache.
Wheel Tier
one level of a hierarchical timing wheel's bucket hierarchy.
Wheel Bucket
one slot within a timer-wheel tier holding pending timer callbacks.
Quarantined Segment
a WAL segment isolated after failing checksum verification.
Late-Join Synchronization
a newly joining receiver's replay-driven catch-up to the live stream.
Virtual Synchrony
the total-order, token-ring membership model this project explicitly rejects at scale (§34 Alt C).
Total Order
a delivery guarantee stronger than this project provides by default (partial order is configurable, §8).
At-Least-Once Delivery
the delivery mode where duplicate suppression is explicitly disabled.
Fail-Closed
the design posture of stopping rather than continuing in an undefined state on invariant violation.
Suppression Cache Shard
the (stream_id, window)-keyed partition of the suppression cache preventing cross-shard duplicate repairs.
