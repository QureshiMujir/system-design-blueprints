Hard questions (§34)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. Why does read repair alone, without active anti-entropy, leave "cold" ranges permanently diverged — and what metric would reveal this in production before an operator notices via a complaint? (§2, §14, §24 FM-1)
  2. Derive the bandwidth cost, in bytes, of reconciling a range with 100 divergent keys via Merkle versus via IBLT at d_expected = 1000, and state which wins and why. (§13.4, §19.4, §33 #5–#6)
  3. Why is the hint queue capped at bytes rather than at hint count, and what regime would make a count-based cap wrong instead? (§9 ADR-09, §33 #3)
  4. Trace, subsystem by subsystem, what happens when a replica returns from a 6-hour partition — name every mechanism that fires and in what order. (§23.3, §14, §15)
  5. Why does synchronous-blocking read repair explode p99 latency, and what does the synchronous-non-blocking design trade away to fix it? (§16.2, §16.4, §6 P6)
  6. Under what conditions does the IBLT fast path fail to decode, and why is that a performance problem here rather than a correctness problem? (§19.2, §19.4, §7 I8)
  7. Why must the LWW timestamp source be an HLC rather than a wall clock, and what failure mode results from getting this wrong? (§18.1, §6 P7, §24 FM-6)
  8. Why are siblings opt-in rather than the global default, and what operational signal indicates a keyspace should switch to a CRDT resolver instead? (§18.2, §6 P8, §24 FM-7)
  9. Why must the token bucket be per-(peer, range) rather than global, and what specific failure does a global bucket permit? (§14.2, §14.6, §6 P13)
  10. Derive the number of gossip rounds needed to disseminate a rumor across a 1000-node cluster, and connect that number to the choice of a 1 Hz gossip cycle. (§5 Papers, §9 ADR-06, §33 #7)
  11. Why is the Deferred Features Register's BFT item not simply "add signatures to every hash," and what trust-boundary assumption would have to change first? (§23.1, §9 ADR-22, §36)
  12. What three distinct persistent-divergence classes result from removing each of the three repair mechanisms individually, and where is each documented? (§6 P2, §24 FM-1, FM-2, FM-3)
  13. Why must the Merkle leaf hash incorporate the HLC timestamp, not just key and value, and what false-convergence bug results if it does not? (§13.6, §13.8)
  14. What does this system gain and lose, relative to a CRDT-only design, by making CRDTs opt-in rather than mandatory? (§6 P9, §32, §35.1)
  15. Why is repair concurrency bounded per node (ADR-08) independently of the token bucket's bandwidth bound, and what happens if only one of the two bounds exists? (§9 ADR-08, §14.2, §4)
  16. What is the practical scaling ceiling of the gossip scheduler as cluster size grows, and what two levers exist beyond it? (§30, §35.3)
  17. Why does the cross-DC gossip cycle use batched-leaf hashing while the intra-DC path does not, and what cost-profile difference justifies this? (§20.2, §13, §33 #10)
  18. What does "hinted handoff without read repair" miss that "read repair without hinted handoff" does not, and vice versa? (§6 P2, §24 FM-2, FM-3)
  19. Why is the conformance suite a merge gate rather than a monitoring dashboard, and what historical audit motivates treating it this way? (§6 P12, §27)
  20. Under a bandwidth-constrained, high-divergence WAN regime, which Alternative Architecture (§35) would outperform this design, and why was it still rejected? (§35, §19, §20)

31. Mental Models & First Principles

  1. MM1 — A Merkle tree is a bandwidth-compression scheme for equality checking. The insight: you never need to ship the data to know whether two ranges match; you ship a 32-byte summary and only descend where summaries disagree. It predicts that fingerprint exchange cost is independent of range size and depends only on divergence (§13.4's O(d log N) bound). The bug class you write without it: unconditionally shipping full range payloads to "be safe," which is the O(N) design this entire subsystem exists to avoid. Resolving §: §13.
  2. MM2 — Repair is a rate, not a state. There is no "fully repaired" signal (P11); the only meaningful question is "what is the reconciliation rate right now, relative to the divergence rate?" It predicts that any dashboard or runbook promising "repair complete" is wrong by construction. The bug class without it: operators who wait for a terminal state that never arrives, or alerts that fire on "repair still running" as if that were itself an anomaly. Resolving §: §14, §30.
  3. MM3 — Gossip convergence time is logarithmic in cluster size, not linear. From Karp-Schenker-Shenker (§5): a rumor started at one node reaches all N nodes in O(log N) push-pull-push rounds. It predicts that a 1 Hz cycle stays adequate as cluster size grows into the low thousands without a cycle-period redesign. The bug class without it: assuming gossip "doesn't scale" and over-engineering a centralized scheduler (P4) that is itself a single point of failure. Resolving §: §14.1, §9 ADR-06.
  4. MM4 — A vector clock without per-writer dots can't tell two independent writers from one replayed write. The Dotted Version Vector insight (Preguiça et al. 2010, §5): annotating each version with a per-replica dot disambiguates "anonymous concurrent update" from "the same update replicated twice." It predicts that a naive vector-clock sibling implementation produces phantom conflicts under replay. The bug class without it: sibling lists that grow even when no real concurrent write occurred. Resolving §: §18.2.
  5. MM5 — IBLT trades a probabilistic guarantee for a communication-complexity win. From Minsky-Trachtenberg-Zippel and Eppstein et al. (§5): O(d) communication is only achievable by accepting a decode-failure probability that grows as the actual difference approaches the provisioned budget. It predicts that any IBLT deployment without a correctness-preserving fallback will eventually — not might, will — fail silently at high divergence. The bug class without it: shipping IBLT alone, no Merkle fallback, and treating a rare decode failure as "it'll never happen in practice." Resolving §: §19, §9 ADR-15.
  6. MM6 — A delete is a write, and forgetting that creates unbounded state growth. A tombstone (§11.2) participates in Merkle hashing and gossip exactly like a live value; it predicts that a system that special-cases deletes as "just remove the bytes" breaks convergence (a delete on one replica must still be reconcilable against a live value on another). The bug class without it: an ever-growing keyspace from ungarbage-collected tombstones (§24, adjacent to FM-10's memory-bloat class). Resolving §: §13.6, §24.
  7. MM7 — Backpressure is a design decision, not an accident. Every bounded queue in this system (hint queue, §15; async-repair queue, §17) drops with telemetry rather than growing — this predicts that "just increase the buffer size" is never the fix for a drop-rate alert; the fix is either reducing the input rate or increasing the drain rate. The bug class without it: an unbounded queue that defers an out-of-memory crash instead of surfacing an operational signal early. Resolving §: §15, §17.
  8. MM8 — A resolver is only safe if it is a CAI (commutative, associative, idempotent) function — which is exactly the algebraic structure a CRDT merge has. This predicts that any custom resolver hook (§18.4) that is not CAI will eventually produce a permanently-diverging sibling set under the right write pattern, not just an occasionally-wrong answer. The bug class without it: a "reasonable-looking" custom merge (e.g., pick-the-longest-value) that silently breaks convergence under replay or reordering. Resolving §: §11.7, §18.

  • Burckhardt, S., 2014 — Principles of Eventual Consistency, Foundations and Trends in Programming Languages 1(1–2). Extract: the CRDT chapters and the commutative/ associative/idempotent (CAI) convergence-proof contract. Applied in §11.7, §18.
  • Tanenbaum, A. S., Van Steen, M., 2007 — Distributed Systems: Principles and Paradigms, 2nd ed., Prentice Hall, ch. 7 ("Consistency and Replication"). Extract: the formal taxonomy of consistency models framing §4's constraint table. Applied in §4.
  • Petrov, A., 2019 — Database Internals, O'Reilly, part II ("Distributed Systems"), ch. 11–12 (replication, consistency, anti-entropy). Extract: the practitioner walk-through of Merkle-tree anti-entropy and hinted handoff in real LSM-based stores. Applied in §13, §15.
  • Cachin, C., Guerraoui, R., Rodrigues, L., 2011 — Introduction to Reliable and Secure Distributed Programming, 2nd ed., Springer, ch. 3 ("Reliable Broadcast") and ch. 6 ("Consensus"). Extract: the formal gossip/epidemic-broadcast model and its convergence bounds. Applied in §14, §31 (MM3).

Reference Implementations

  • Apache Cassandra — org.apache.cassandra.repair, org.apache.cassandra.service.reads.repair. Study: the incremental-repair design (CASSANDRA-5351) and read_repair_chance wiring. Lesson: repair scope must be topology-aware (§14.2). Pitfall not to copy: pre-2017 synchronous-blocking read repair by default (CASSANDRA-13289 fixed it). Applied in §16.4 as the negative example motivating the synchronous-non-blocking default.
  • Riak — riak_kv_aae_tree, riak_kv_hinted_handoff. Study: the Active Anti-Entropy hash-tree exchange and allow_mult sibling semantics. Lesson: siblings must be opt-in per keyspace (§18.2). Pitfall not to copy: unbounded hint accumulation in early Riak releases before spill-to-disk was added. Applied in §15.
  • ScyllaDB — repair.cc, hinted_handoff.cc. Study: the C++/seastar repair scheduler's shard-aware concurrency model. Lesson: repair concurrency must be bounded per shard, not just per node (informs ADR-08). Applied in §14.
  • CockroachDB — pkg/kv/kvserver/replica_consistency.go. Study: the periodic consistency checksum protocol (a Merkle-adjacent design over Raft-replicated ranges). Lesson: checksum granularity trades detection latency for CPU. Applied in §32 (comparative table).
  • Amazon DynamoDB (as documented in the 2007 Dynamo paper and subsequent public re-architecture writeups) — no public source; studied via its own paper's sec. 4.6/4.7. Lesson: sloppy quorum plus hinted handoff plus Merkle anti-entropy shipped together from day one, not incrementally. Applied in §2, §12.

RFCs / Specifications

  • RocksDB documentation — Column Families, Write-Ahead Log. Applied in §15.2 (hint persistence layout, ADR-05).
  • CBOR (RFC 8949) — Concise Binary Object Representation. Applied in §8.1 (wire format, ADR-16).

Glossary (§37) · 42 terms · site-wide glossary

Active anti-entropy
the gossip-scheduled, Merkle-driven background reconciliation mechanism that repairs ranges nobody is reading or hinting about (§14).
Anti-entropy
the general class of protocols that detect and repair replica divergence, as opposed to preventing it (Demers et al. 1987).
Asynchronous (probabilistic) read repair
repair triggered on a sampled fraction of reads, fired-and-forgotten to a background task (§17).
Backpressure
the deliberate rejection or dropping of new work, with telemetry, once a bounded queue is full, rather than letting the queue grow unboundedly (§15, §17).
Bounded staleness
a quantified upper bound on how out-of-date a read may be, expressible as P(staleness > t) (Bailis et al. 2012 PBS, §33).
CAI contract
commutative, associative, idempotent: the algebraic property every Resolver implementation must satisfy for convergence to be provable (I6, §11.7).
Cell
a fixed-size slot in an IBLT holding XOR-accumulated element IDs and a check hash plus a signed count (§11.9).
Check hash
a BLAKE3-keyed hash stored per IBLT cell, used to verify a cell is "pure" during peel-back decoding without a false positive (§19.2).
Column family (CF)
a RocksDB namespace-within-a-database; used one-per-target- replica in the hint queue for efficient bounded eviction and replay (§11.10).
Conflict resolution
the process of reducing multiple divergent VersionedValues for one key down to a single resolved outcome (§18).
Conformance suite
the deterministic property-test/fixture runner that gates every merge and release (§27.1).
Consistency level
the (N, W, R) quorum configuration a read or write is executed under (§16.2).
Divergent leaf
a Merkle-tree leaf whose hash differs between two replicas being compared, localizing the set of keys that actually need reconciliation (§13.2).
Dotted Version Vector (DVV)
a vector-clock variant that disambiguates anonymous concurrent updates from replayed ones via per-replica dots (Preguiça et al. 2010, §18.2).
Domain separation
using distinct hash-function prefixes for structurally different purposes (e.g., leaf vs. internal-node hashing) to prevent cross-context collisions (§13.6).
Eventual consistency
a consistency model guaranteeing that, absent new writes, all replicas converge to the same state in bounded time (Vogels 2009).
Fingerprint
a compact summary (Merkle root + metadata) exchanged in place of full range payloads (§11.3).
Fingerprint-first, payload-second
this system's core bandwidth strategy: exchange cheap summaries aggressively, ship expensive payloads only once divergence is localized (§3).
Generation
a monotonically increasing counter on a RangeFingerprint guarding against comparing fingerprints across incompatible range/topology states (§11.3, §8.4).
Gossip
a peer-to-peer, decentralized information-dissemination protocol with logarithmic convergence time in cluster size (Demers et al. 1987, Karp et al. 2000).
Hinted handoff
buffering a write intended for a temporarily-unreachable replica at the coordinator, for later replay ("sloppy quorum," Dynamo sec. 4.6, §15).
Hint
a single buffered (target, key, versioned_value) write awaiting replay (§11.5).
Hybrid Logical Clock (HLC)
a clock combining a physical-time component with a logical counter, giving a total order that respects causality when observable (Kulkarni et al. 2014, §18.1).
Idempotent write-back
a repair write that is a safe no-op if the target already holds an equal-or-newer value, enforced via HLC comparison at the storage layer (§16.2).
Invertible Bloom Lookup Table (IBLT)
a probabilistic data structure supporting exact recovery of a bounded symmetric set difference via peel-back decoding (Eppstein et al. 2011, §19).
Last-Writer-Wins (LWW)
a conflict-resolution policy that always picks the value with the highest timestamp (§18.1).
Load amplification (repair-induced)
a failure mode where repair traffic itself worsens the condition (e.g., a partial outage) it was meant to fix (P13, §24 FM-5).
Merkle tree
a hash tree enabling O(log N)-depth equality verification of a data set via recursive hash comparison (Merkle 1987, §13).
Peel-back decoding
the IBLT decode algorithm that iteratively removes "pure" cells until the structure is empty or exhausted (§19.2).
Policy registry
the gossip-distributed, per-keyspace configuration store for resolver choice, d_expected, and cap/TTL overrides (§21.1).
Quorum
the minimum number of replicas (W for writes, R for reads) that must respond for an operation to be considered successful, out of N total (§16.2).
RangeId
a stable identifier for a (range, replica-set) pair that survives membership/topology changes even as the underlying key boundaries shift (§11.1).
Read repair
request-time detection and correction of replica divergence on the keys a client is actually reading (§16, §17).
Resolver
the pluggable trait implementing conflict resolution; implementors include LWW, Siblings, CRDT, and application-defined hooks (§11.7).
Sloppy quorum
accepting a write as successful once W replicas (not necessarily the "natural" N owners) acknowledge it, buffering the rest as hints (Dynamo sec. 4.6, §15.1).
Sibling
one of several mutually-concurrent VersionedValues returned to the caller when the resolver cannot or should not pick a single winner (§18.2).
Symmetric difference
the set of elements present in exactly one of two sets being reconciled (A △ B), the quantity IBLT and Merkle diff both aim to recover (§19).
Synchronous read repair
read-time repair whose write-back either blocks the client response (rejected default) or completes in the background before or after the resolved value is returned (adopted default, §16).
Token bucket
a rate-limiting structure that caps repair bandwidth per (peer, range) pair, replenished at a fixed rate (§14.2, I10).
Tombstone
a VersionedValue marking a deletion, retained and garbage-collected on a TTL rather than removed immediately, so deletes participate correctly in convergence (§11.2, §13.6).
VersionedValue
the canonical (value, timestamp, tombstone-flag) triple every stored entry carries (§11.2).
Write-back
the repair-driven write that applies a resolved value to a replica found to be missing or behind it (§16.2).
