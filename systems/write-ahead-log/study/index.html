Hard questions (§33)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. Why must a page's modified bytes never reach durable storage before the log record describing that modification is forced, and what exactly breaks if the order is reversed? (§6 P1, §7 I3, §14)
  2. Why does ARIES redo updates belonging to transactions that will later be undone, rather than skipping them once a transaction is known to be a loser? (§6 P2, §20)
  3. Why is an undo action itself logged as a Compensation Log Record instead of simply being re-executed from the original update's inverse? (§6 P3, §7 I8, §20)
  4. What specific field in a CLR prevents an undo action from being repeated across multiple crashes during rollback, and how does Analysis reconstruct it correctly after such a crash? (§7 I8, §19.6, §8.7)
  5. Why can a fuzzy checkpoint safely avoid flushing any dirty pages, when a sharp checkpoint cannot? (§6 P10, §9 ADR-08, §18)
  6. Why does the Analysis pass need to start from a checkpoint's begin_checkpoint_lsn rather than its CHECKPOINT_END LSN? (§7 I10, §18.6, §19)
  7. What would go wrong if a corrupted record's declared length were trusted enough to skip past it and resume scanning, instead of truncating the log there? (§6 P8, §17)
  8. Why does this design choose physiological logging over pure physical (full page image) logging, and under what payload-size regime would the pure-physical choice actually be better? (§6 P6, §9 ADR-12)
  9. Why is single-writer serialization (ADR-19) not simply a performance limitation to be "fixed" with more writer threads, and what specific guarantee depends on it? (§6 P4, §29 bottleneck #2, §30 MM7)
  10. Why does group commit trade the fastest committer's latency for aggregate throughput, and what would happen to M1/M2 if the batching window were removed entirely? (§6 P9, §28 M1/M2/M9)
  11. Why must retention wait for both checkpoint completion and every replica's acknowledgment, rather than either condition alone? (§6 P12, §7 I11, §21)
  12. Trace the exact sequence of log writes and page-flush events that distinguishes "crash before the commit record was forced" from "crash after, before the page was flushed" — why do these two crash points require different recovery actions? (§8.4–§8.5, §14.4)
  13. Why does RocksDB's WAL need no undo pass at all, while this project's WAL fundamentally requires one? (§31, §7 I7/I8)
  14. Why is SQLite's single-growing-WAL-file design a legitimate alternative to segment rotation rather than a simplification that loses correctness? (§31, §9 ADR-06)
  15. Why does a checkpoint interval that is too long threaten §4's RTO target, and why does a checkpoint interval that is too short threaten §28's steady-state latency targets — what is the actual tradeoff curve? (§18.7, §29, §32 item 4–5)
  16. Why is CRC-32C sufficient for this project's corruption-detection goals but explicitly insufficient against an adversarial actor, and what would replace it if that threat model changed? (§7 I4, §22 T10, §34)
  17. Why does a replica apply records through the same DurableWriter append path as the primary, rather than through a separate, simpler "just write these bytes" mechanism? (§21.2, §30 MM8)
  18. What is the precise difference between "the log's true end" as determined by wal-reader (Phase 5) and "the log's declared end" as determined by the last segment's trailer — and why must recovery trust the former over the latter? (§17.3, §8.6)
  19. Why does this design's recovery pipeline need to be re-runnable from scratch after a crash during recovery itself, and what property of Redo and Undo makes that safe? (§19.9, §20.9, §6 P14)
  20. Why would shadow paging (§34) eliminate the need for a redo pass entirely, and what does it give up in exchange? (§34, §6 P1/P5)
  21. Why is the log-structured-storage alternative (§34, Rosenblum & Ousterhout 1992) not simply "a WAL that never gets checkpointed into separate pages," and what different problem does it actually solve? (§34, §31)
  22. Why does this project treat "torn write" and "clean segment boundary" as fundamentally different signals that must never be confused, even though both involve "no more valid bytes here"? (§17.6, §7 I4)

30. Mental Models & First Principles

  • MM1 — "The log is the truth; the page is a cache of it." Compressed insight: a data page's on-disk bytes are only ever a performance optimization over replaying the log — the log alone is sufficient to reconstruct any page (P1/P2). Predicts: any design that can lose a page but not the log recovers fully; any design that can lose log bytes but keep the page cannot be trusted. Bug class without it: flushing pages "for safety" ahead of their log records (FM-1). Resolving §: §6 (P1), §7 (I3).

  • MM2 — "Durability is a property of bytes on disk, not of code having run." Compressed insight: force() completing is the only event that matters for durability — everything before it is provisional regardless of how much code executed. Predicts: any "we already applied it in memory" reasoning about durability is a category error. Bug class without it: acknowledging a commit before its force() completes (I2 violation). Resolving §: §6 (P1/P9), §14.

  • MM3 — "Redo doesn't ask who wins; undo does." Compressed insight: repeating history (P2) applies every update uniformly, deferring the winner/loser question entirely to the Undo pass — this separation is what makes Redo simple and idempotent. Predicts: any recovery design trying to skip redoing "probably-losing" transactions has conflated two separate questions. Bug class without it: pages left in a state inconsistent with the log's physical history (I6 violation). Resolving §: §6 (P2), §20.

  • MM4 — "Undo must be as durable as the thing it's undoing." Compressed insight: because undo can itself be interrupted by a crash, an undo action must be logged (CLR) with the same durability discipline as a forward update, or rollback isn't actually crash-safe. Predicts: any "just re-run the inverse operation" rollback scheme is unsafe under crash-during-rollback. Bug class without it: undo livelock across repeated crashes (FM-5). Resolving §: §6 (P3), §7 (I8), §20.

  • MM5 — "A checkpoint is a hint, not a commitment." Compressed insight: a fuzzy checkpoint only shortens where Analysis starts scanning — it never claims the snapshotted pages are actually flushed (P10). Predicts: any code path that treats "checkpoint completed" as "data is now safe without the log" is wrong. Bug class without it: deleting log segments based on checkpoint completion alone, ignoring replica acknowledgment (P12 violation, FM-9). Resolving §: §6 (P10/P12), §18.

  • MM6 — "Corruption detection and corruption repair are different problems, and this system only solves the first." Compressed insight: a CRC tells you a record is wrong; it never tells you what the record should have been (P7/P8). Predicts: any "smart recovery" that tries to reconstruct a corrupted record's likely original content is solving a problem this design deliberately declines to solve. Bug class without it: skip-and-resume-scanning logic that silently misinterprets subsequent bytes (explicitly rejected, §17.6). Resolving §: §6 (P7/P8), §17.

  • MM7 — "One writer, many readers, is not a limitation — it's what makes ordering free." Compressed insight: LSN monotonicity (P4/I1) is trivial to guarantee with a single serialization point, and everything downstream (redo order, undo order, segment addressing) depends on that ordering being unconditionally true. Predicts: any attempt to parallelize the writer without a new ordering mechanism reintroduces exactly the bugs P4 exists to prevent. Bug class without it: LSN races corrupting redo/undo direction. Resolving §: §6 (P4), §29 (bottleneck #2).

  • MM8 — "Replication is just another reader with a stricter retention contract." Compressed insight: a log-shipping receiver is structurally the same DurableWriter append path as the primary, fed remotely — the only new concept is that retention now has to wait for it too (P12). Predicts: replication bugs are almost always retention-gate bugs, not streaming-protocol bugs. Bug class without it: premature segment deletion stranding a replica (FM-9). Resolving §: §6 (P12), §21.

Required reading (§5)

Books

  • Gray, J. & Reuter, A., Transaction Processing: Concepts and Techniques (Morgan Kaufmann, 1992) — logging and recovery chapters covering log managers, checkpoint design, and the force/no-force, steal/no-steal taxonomy. Applied in §6 (P1/P5) / Phase 2 (§14).
  • Hellerstein, J., Stonebraker, M. & Hamilton, J., Architecture of a Database System, Foundations and Trends in Databases 1(2) (2007) — §6 "Transactions: Concurrency Control and Recovery" covers the log manager's role alongside the buffer manager. Applied in §3 / Phase 6 (§18).
  • Silberschatz, A., Korth, H. & Sudarshan, S., Database System Concepts (7th ed., McGraw-Hill, 2019), ch. 19 "Recovery System" — extract: WAL rule statement, log record formats, checkpoint mechanics, ARIES overview. Applied in §7 / Phase 7 (§19).
  • Ramakrishnan, R. & Gehrke, J., Database Management Systems (3rd ed., McGraw-Hill, 2003), ch. 18 "Crash Recovery" — extract: the canonical worked ARIES walkthrough (analysis/redo/undo tables). Applied in Phase 7 (§19) / Phase 8 (§20).
  • Bernstein, P., Hadzilacos, V. & Goodman, N., Concurrency Control and Recovery in Database Systems (Addison-Wesley, 1987), ch. 6–7 "Log-Based Recovery" — extract: formal treatment of log-based vs. shadow-based recovery. Applied in §34 / §6 (P1).
  • Weikum, G. & Vossen, G., Transactional Information Systems (Morgan Kaufmann, 2001), ch. 6 "Log-Based Database Recovery" — extract: DPT/TT bookkeeping structures and checkpoint-interval tradeoffs. Applied in Phase 6 (§18) / §29.

Papers

  • Mohan, C., Haderle, D., Lindsay, B., Pirahesh, H. & Schwarz, P. (1992). "ARIES: A Transaction Recovery Method Supporting Fine-Granularity Locking and Partial Rollbacks Using Write-Ahead Logging." ACM TODS 17(1):94–162 — the canonical WAL/ recovery algorithm this project implements. Applied in §6 (P1–P3) / Phase 7–8 (§19–§20).
  • Härder, T. & Reuter, A. (1983). "Principles of Transaction-Oriented Database Recovery." ACM Computing Surveys 15(4):287–317 — defines the WAL rule and the force/no-force, steal/no-steal recovery taxonomy by name. Applied in §6 (P1/P5) / §11.
  • Rosenblum, M. & Ousterhout, J. (1992). "The Design and Implementation of a Log-Structured File System." ACM TOCS 10(1):26–52 — the log-as-only-storage alternative architecture contrasted against WAL-plus-pages in §34. Applied in §34.
  • Mohan, C. & Levine, F. (1992). "ARIES/IM: An Efficient and High Concurrency Index Management Method Using Write-Ahead Logging." ACM SIGMOD — extends ARIES to index structural modifications; the source for treating structure modifications as a distinct redo/undo case. Applied in Phase 8 (§20) / §8.
  • Lomet, D. & Tuttle, M. (1995). "Redo Recovery after System Crashes." VLDB — formal proof obligations for redo-pass correctness under repeating history. Applied in Phase 8 (§20) / §7 (I6).
  • Gray, J. (1978). "Notes on Data Base Operating Systems." In Operating Systems: An Advanced Course, LNCS 60, Springer — early statement of the DO-UNDO-REDO log protocol that ARIES generalizes. Applied in §2 / §6 (P2).
  • Mohan, C. (1999). "Repeating History Beyond ARIES." VLDB (invited talk) — retrospective on ARIES's design choices and where later systems diverged from them. Applied in §31 / §34.
  • Lampson, B. & Sturgis, H. (1979). "Crash Recovery in a Distributed Data Storage System." Xerox PARC technical report — early formal comparison of logging vs. shadowing for crash recovery. Applied in §34.

Reference Implementations

  • PostgreSQL, src/backend/access/transam/xlog.c and xlogrecovery.c — study the physical WAL record format, checkpoint records, and the recovery redo loop. Lesson: fixed-size (16 MiB default) segment files named by a monotonic hex ID. Pitfall: WAL retention misconfiguration (wal_keep_size) can either bloat disk or strand a replica — mirrors FM-9. Applied in §31 / Phase 4 (§16).
  • SQLite, src/wal.c, documented at sqlite.org/wal.html — study the single growing -wal file design and checkpoint-triggered reset. Lesson: a WAL need not rotate through many files if checkpoint frequency is high enough. Pitfall: readers must use the WAL index to find the latest committed frame for a page, or they see stale data. Applied in §31 / ADR-06.
  • Facebook RocksDB, db/log_writer.cc / db/log_reader.cc / db/log_format.h — study the 7-byte record header (length + type + CRC32C) and 32 KiB physical block framing. Lesson: logical (key/value) records can still use a physical block/CRC framing identical in spirit to a page-oriented WAL. Pitfall: a record split across block boundaries needs FIRST/MIDDLE/LAST fragment types, easy to get wrong. Applied in §31 / Phase 1 (§13) / Phase 5 (§17).
  • Google LevelDB, db/log_writer.cc / db/log_reader.cc — the predecessor to RocksDB's WAL format; study the same block-framing scheme in a smaller codebase. Lesson: the format is simple enough to read start-to-finish in an afternoon. Applied in Phase 1 (§13).
  • etcd, server/storage/wal package (wal.go) — study segment-file naming (%016x-%016x.wal), per-record CRC chaining, and the metadata/snapshot-based truncation trigger. Lesson: the same append/CRC/segment-rotate skeleton serves a consensus (Raft) log, not just a page-store log. Pitfall: CRC chaining (each record's CRC seeded from the previous) means truncating mid-file requires recomputing the chain, not just dropping bytes. Applied in §31 / Phase 9 (§21).
  • MySQL/InnoDB, storage/innobase/log/log0log.cc — study the circular redo-log buffer and checkpoint LSN (checkpoint_lsn) bookkeeping. Lesson: a fixed-size circular log (vs. rotating discrete segments) is a valid alternative segment model. Applied in §29 / ADR-06.

RFCs / Specifications

  • IEEE Std 1003.1-2017 (POSIX.1), fsync()/fdatasync() system call semantics — defines the durability guarantee (and its filesystem-metadata-vs-data distinction) this project's force() barrier is built on. Applied in §6 (P1) / Phase 2 (§14) / ADR-04.
  • SQLite "Write-Ahead Logging" specification (sqlite.org/wal.html) — describes the WAL-index and checkpoint-reset protocol referenced as a comparative design in §31. Applied in §31.

Glossary (§36) · 35 terms · site-wide glossary

ARIES
Algorithm for Recovery and Isolation Exploiting Semantics; the three-pass (Analysis/Redo/Undo) recovery method this project implements (Mohan et al. 1992).
Analysis pass
the ARIES pass that rebuilds the Dirty Page Table and Transaction Table from a checkpoint forward to the log's true end (§8.5, Phase 7).
CLR (Compensation Log Record)
a log record describing an undo action, carrying an UndoNextLSN so rollback is idempotent under repeated crashes (P3).
Checkpoint
a periodic snapshot of recovery-relevant state used to bound Analysis-pass scan length (P10).
CRC-32C
the cyclic redundancy check variant used for per-record corruption detection in this project (ADR-03).
Dirty Page Table (DPT)
the table of pages modified but not yet flushed since their earliest unflushed update, keyed by rec_lsn (§8.3).
fdatasync
the POSIX syscall flushing a file's data (and only the metadata needed to read it back) to durable storage; this project's default durability barrier (ADR-04, POSIX.1-2017).
fsync
the POSIX syscall flushing a file's data and all metadata to durable storage; used as a fallback when fdatasync is unavailable (ADR-04).
Force / force()
this project's durability barrier abstraction wrapping fdatasync/fsync (P1).
Fuzzy checkpoint
a checkpoint that snapshots bookkeeping state without flushing dirty pages, avoiding an I/O stall (ADR-08).
Group commit
batching multiple transactions' commit records into a single force() call (P9).
LSN (Log Sequence Number)
the strictly monotonic identifier assigned to every log record in append order (P4, I1).
LSM tree (Log-Structured Merge tree)
an alternative storage architecture with no in-place page to redo/undo against, contrasted in §34.
Loser transaction
a transaction active or aborting at crash time, requiring undo during recovery (§8.3).
No-force policy
the policy that a committed transaction's dirty pages need not be flushed at commit time, relying on redo capability instead (P5).
No-steal policy
the (rejected, ADR-09) policy that uncommitted pages are never evicted from the buffer pool before commit, eliminating the need for undo.
pageLSN
the LSN of the last update applied to a given data page, used for the redo-skip check (I6, ADR-13).
Payload
the record-type-specific bytes following a RecordHeader (§11).
Physiological logging
logging that names a page physically but describes the operation logically, rather than storing full page images (P6, ADR-12).
Redo pass
the ARIES pass that reapplies every logged update not yet reflected in its target page, regardless of the owning transaction's eventual fate (P2).
RecordHeader
the fixed 32-byte structure prefixing every log record's payload (§11).
RecordType
the tag distinguishing Begin/Update/Commit/Abort/Clr/ CheckpointBegin/CheckpointEnd records (§11).
Repeating history
the principle that redo reapplies all logged updates uniformly, deferring winner/loser distinction to undo (P2, MM3).
Retention gate
the mechanism blocking segment deletion until covered by both a checkpoint and every replica's acknowledgment (P12, ADR-16).
Segment (file)
a fixed-size (16 MiB) chunk of the logical, unbounded log (ADR-06, P11).
Shadow paging
an alternative, log-free recovery architecture using copy-on-write page tables, contrasted in §34.
SegmentHeader
the magic/version/ID/first-LSN header at the start of every segment file (§8.1).
Single-writer discipline
the rule that exactly one thread ever appends to the log, enabling group commit and LSN ordering without locking (ADR-19, P4).
Steal policy
the policy that the buffer pool may evict uncommitted (dirty) pages before their owning transaction commits, requiring undo capability (ADR-09/11).
Torn write
a partially-completed physical write, typically from a crash mid-write, detected via CRC/length mismatch (P7, §8.6).
Transaction Table (TT)
the table of transactions active (or aborting) at a given point, tracking last_lsn and undo_next_lsn (§8.3).
Truncate-tail policy
the rule that a torn/corrupt tail is dropped, never repaired or partially skipped (P8).
Undo pass
the ARIES pass that rolls back every loser transaction's updates via CLRs, in strict reverse-LSN order (P3, I7).
UndoNextLSN
the field in a CLR (or Transaction Table entry) pointing to the next record still needing undo, guaranteeing idempotent rollback (I8).
WAL protocol / WAL rule
the rule that a log record must be durable before the data it describes is written to durable storage (Härder & Reuter 1983, P1). ---
