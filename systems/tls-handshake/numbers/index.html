34. Back-of-Envelope Capacity Model

Full-handshake CPU cost, decomposed (from §4/§30's M7-M11): X25519 ECDHE (~50 μs)

  • signature generation, server-side ECDSA-P256 (~50 μs, asymmetric-cheaper-than- verify) + signature verification, client-side ECDSA-P256 (~80 μs) + certificate-chain parse/validate for a 3-cert chain (~30 μs) ≈ 210 microseconds of pure crypto/parse cost per full handshake (server-side view; client-side is similar with roles swapped). At 210 μs/handshake, one core's theoretical ceiling is 1,000,000 / 210 ≈ 4,760 handshakes/second/core, matching M1's 3,000-4,500 target once scheduling/allocation overhead (roughly 10-25%) is subtracted.

Resumed-handshake CPU cost (symmetric only): HKDF-Extract/Expand chain (~5 μs total across the schedule) + AEAD key derivation (~2 μs) + Finished HMAC verify (~2 μs) ≈ ~10 microseconds per resumed handshake. Ceiling: 1,000,000 / 10 = 100,000 handshakes/second/core theoretical, matching M2's 40,000-60,000 target once overhead is subtracted. Ratio: resumption is roughly 20-25x cheaper than a full handshake — the arithmetic that motivates ADR-14/ADR-15 and Phase 10's entire existence.

Record-layer throughput from AEAD cost: AES-128-GCM with AES-NI hardware acceleration runs at roughly 0.5-1 cycle/byte; at a 3 GHz core, that is 3,000,000,000 / 0.75 ≈ 4 GB/s theoretical, matching M5's 2,000-4,000 MB/s target once framing/copy overhead is subtracted. Without AES-NI, software AES-GCM costs roughly 5-8 cycles/byte, dropping to 400-600 MB/s — this is exactly why ChaCha20-Poly1305 (software-optimized to ~2-3 cycles/byte, no hardware dependency, ~1,000-1,500 MB/s at 3 GHz) is preferred absent AES-NI (Phase 3 N.7, M6).

Nonce space and the practical rekey point. The nonce space is 2^64 records (§8.3's 64-bit sequence number). AES-GCM's safety margin (NIST SP 800-38D) recommends rekeying well before 2^32 records under a single key for the strongest confidentiality bound in adversarial settings; at a sustained 4 GB/s (M5) and an average 1,400-byte record, 2^32 records is 2^32 * 1,400 bytes ≈ 6 TB of data before a conservative rekey, comfortably beyond any single connection's realistic lifetime but explicitly enforced by I2's rekey-before-wraparound guard as a defense-in-depth measure, not a practically-reachable limit in normal operation.

Per-connection memory (buffers + key schedule + certificate state): two TrafficKeys (32-byte key + 12-byte IV each, both directions, roughly 200 bytes with overhead) + transcript-hash state (~100 bytes) + a 3-certificate chain retained for the connection's lifetime for potential renegotiation-adjacent diagnostics (~3-6 KB typical) + record-layer buffers sized to the max record (2^14+256 bytes, both directions ≈ 33 KB) ≈ roughly 36-40 KB worst case, consistent with M12's 16-32 KB target for a connection that has dropped its certificate-chain retention post- handshake (the more common steady-state figure).

Handshake bandwidth (certificate chain dominates the ServerHello flight): a typical 3-certificate chain (leaf + intermediate + root, each 1-2 KB DER-encoded) is roughly 3-6 KB, dwarfing the rest of the handshake's message bytes (ClientHello/ ServerHello/EncryptedExtensions/Finished together total well under 1 KB) — this is why certificate-chain size is a first-class §4 constraint (T10) and why certificate compression (RFC 8879, PhaseDeferred) is a real bandwidth optimization for high-latency/low-bandwidth links.

0-RTT savings at a stated RTT: at a representative 50 ms round-trip time, a 1-RTT handshake costs 2 round trips of wall-clock latency before application data flows (100 ms) versus 0-RTT's 1 round trip (50 ms) — a 50 ms savings per connection, which compounds significantly for a client establishing many short-lived connections (e.g. a mobile client reconnecting frequently), the concrete number behind ADR-15's "0-RTT's latency win is real" claim.
