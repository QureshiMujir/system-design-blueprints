Hard questions (§35)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. If auth-service is down but its introspection cache at the gateway is still warm, what exactly is the platform's actual availability during that window, and how does that interact with the composed-SLO arithmetic? (§14, §34)
  2. Why does the platform commit to a 99.5% edge SLO when the composed-availability arithmetic already lands at 99.5% with zero margin at five hops — what happens the day one hop underperforms its own SLO? (§26, §34)
  3. Given I12 only guarantees ordering within a partition key, how does search-service's projection ever reconcile two events for the same job routed to different partitions by a partition-key-assignment bug? (§8, §16)
  4. If a saga's compensation action is itself not idempotent (FM-6) and the compensation event is redelivered while an on-call engineer is manually patching the handler, what prevents the manual fix and the automatic redelivery from racing? (§19, §25)
  5. The blast-radius bound (I9) caps degraded dependents at 3 — does that invariant even apply cleanly to auth-service, the one service every other service depends on, or does I9 implicitly assume a bounded fan-out that auth-service violates by design? (§7, §14)
  6. Why is the schema-registry compatibility table's "none" mode allowed at all as a major version bump rather than rejected outright — doesn't permitting it strain P3's additive-only principle? (§6, §8)
  7. Given database-per-service (P1) and the 120-connections-per-service budget, what actually happens when a 28th service is added and the platform crosses toward the documented ceiling — does §31's coordination-overhead argument or §34's connection-math argument bind first? (§31, §34)
  8. If cache-service fails open and every dependent service falls back to direct DB reads simultaneously during an incident, does the resulting DB load spike itself trigger a second, cascading incident — and does I9's blast-radius bound account for this second-order effect? (§20, §25)
  9. The platform chooses choreography-first and orchestration only for complex sagas (P4) — what is the actual decision procedure for classifying a workflow as complex enough to warrant an orchestrator, and who owns that call when two teams disagree? (§6, §19)
  10. Given a single saga spans multiple aggregates with different partition keys, how does the saga orchestrator avoid becoming an implicit, informally-specified second ordering mechanism the platform never formally documents? (§8, §9)
  11. Why does ADR-19 defer the service mesh past the 40-service ceiling rather than adopting it now, given that mTLS and retries are currently handled per-application — does this mean 27 different retry/mTLS implementations across 21 languages, and how is consistency of that behavior actually verified? (§9, §28)
  12. The composed-availability arithmetic assumes independent per-service failures — how wrong is that model when a single shared dependency (the Kubernetes control plane, a cloud provider's network layer) causes correlated failures across multiple services simultaneously? (§34, §25)
  13. If a 27th service's contract tests pass in isolation but the Jepsen-style consistency check finds a genuine violation of the §8 consistency taxonomy only under a specific partition pattern, is the remediation a service rollback or a revision of the taxonomy itself? (§23, §28)
  14. P13's independent-deployability guarantee is verified per-pair — does the platform have any mechanism to catch a three-way coupling where no pair individually violates independent deployability but all three together do? (§6, §23)
  15. Why does the error-budget policy freeze deploys per-service rather than platform-wide — what stops a service with an exhausted budget from having its traffic quietly rerouted at the gateway, technically frozen but effectively hiding the violation from users? (§26, §15)
  16. The trace-storage arithmetic assumes a fixed 10% sampling rate — what breaks first at 10x traffic growth without revisiting that rate, debuggability of a rare-tail incident or the Jaeger storage budget, and which section owns the decision to change it? (§34, §22)
  17. Given the platform rejects two-phase commit (ADR-09) in favor of sagas, is there any cross-service transaction among the 27 services that structurally requires atomicity sagas cannot provide, and if so, why wasn't it flagged as a non-goal in §1? (§9, §1)
  18. If the affected-service detector has a false negative for a genuinely cross-cutting change, and the gap isn't caught until the Phase-11 conformance suite runs, how many services could ship a breaking change before the safety net catches it, and does that number exceed the blast-radius bound in I9? (§12, §23)
  19. The idempotency-key dedupe window is described as "sized to the maximum plausible redelivery lag" (§8) — what actual failure mode results if a redelivery arrives after the window closes, and does I4 still hold in that case, or does it silently stop holding? (§8, §7)
  20. Given ADR-11 forbids search-service from accepting direct writes, how does a compliance-driven takedown (a record that must disappear from search immediately, not on the next projection cycle) get honored without violating the projection-only boundary? (§9, §24)
  21. §31 names organizational scaling (Conway's Law) as a bottleneck distinct from the technical ones — but the platform's phase arc (§12-23) was designed and sequenced before any team structure existed to build it; does the phase ordering itself implicitly assume a team topology, and if so, which one? (§31, §12)
  22. The M15/M16 latency targets for outbox-publish and saga-compensation were set before Phase 6/7 were built — §30's methodology says targets should follow baseline measurement, so were these two targets set correctly, or do they need the same measure-first correction §22.6's pitfall warns against for SLOs? (§30, §22)

32. Mental Models & First Principles

MM1. A microservice boundary is a schema-evolution boundary — if two services must change together, they are one service wearing two deployments. Predicts: any two services whose contract tests fail without a simultaneous deploy are miscarved. Bug class without it: the distributed monolith (FM-1). Resolved by §6 (P13), §23.

MM2. At-least-once delivery plus idempotent consumers is the only exactly-once anyone actually ships. Predicts: any claim of "exactly-once" without an idempotency key is false somewhere. Bug class without it: duplicate effects under retry (I4). Resolved by §8, §18.

MM3. A synchronous call chain's latency is bounded by its slowest hop times its depth, not its average hop. Predicts: a five-hop chain of otherwise-fast services can still miss its p99 budget if any one hop has a fat tail. Bug class without it: tail-latency amplification (Dean & Barroso). Resolved by §7 (I5), §34.

MM4. A saga's compensation is a transaction in its own right and needs everything a transaction needs, including idempotency. Predicts: any "just undo it" compensation handler eventually double-applies under retry. Bug class without it: FM-6. Resolved by §8, §19.

MM5. Consistency is a property of a data class, not of "the system" as a whole — different aggregates on the same platform can and should make different consistency promises. Predicts: a platform-wide "we are eventually consistent" statement is meaningless without naming which data. Bug class without it: mismatched user expectations across features. Resolved by §8 (consistency taxonomy).

MM6. Observability is a query interface into the past, not a dashboard — if you can't ask what service X saw at trace_id Y, you don't have observability, you have monitoring. Predicts: an incident that "should have been visible" but wasn't traces to a missing trace-context propagation point, not a missing dashboard. Bug class without it: FM-7. Resolved by §7 (I3), §22.

MM7. Every partition key is a bet about what will be hot — choose the aggregate that scales with your actual traffic distribution, not the one that's convenient to compute. Predicts: any partition key chosen for developer convenience rather than traffic shape eventually produces a hot partition. Bug class without it: FM-5. Resolved by §9 (ADR-07), §31.

MM8. A blast radius is a graph-connectivity property, not a severity label — you bound it by cutting edges (circuit breakers), not by hoping failures stay small. Predicts: any incident that "spread further than expected" traces to a missing or mis-scoped circuit breaker, not bad luck. Bug class without it: FM-10. Resolved by §7 (I9), §15.

MM9. A wall clock tells you when, never in what order — causal order across services comes only from something that was itself passed between them (an offset, a happens-before token), never from comparing timestamps. Predicts: any cross-service ordering decision keyed on the time field of two independently-produced events is unsound the moment clocks drift. Bug class without it: FM-11. Resolved by §9 (ADR-23), §7 (I12).

MM10. An SLO you haven't measured yet is a guess wearing a number — locking a target before observing baseline behavior produces a budget that is either trivially met or structurally unmeetable, never a real signal. Predicts: any SLO defined before a full traffic cycle of measurement will need revision within one quarter. Bug class without it: alert fatigue from a meaningless threshold, or false confidence from an unearned one. Resolved by §22 (Phase 10), §26.

Required reading (§5)

Books

  • Kleppmann, Designing Data-Intensive Applications — ch. 5 (Replication), ch. 7 (Transactions), ch. 9 (Consistency and Consensus), ch. 11 (Stream Processing). Extract: exactly-once end-to-end doesn't exist, only effectively-once via idempotency; log-based CDC as the outbox mechanism. Applied in §18 (Phase 6).
  • Newman, Building Microservices, 2nd ed. — ch. 3 (service boundaries), ch. 11 (testing), ch. 14 (organizational scaling). Extract: bounded-context-aligned ownership; consumer-driven contracts. Applied in §6, §28.
  • Richardson, Microservices Patterns — ch. 3 (transactional messaging/outbox), ch. 4 (sagas), ch. 11 (deployment). Extract: choreography vs orchestration tradeoffs; outbox mechanics. Applied in §19 (Phase 7).
  • Nygard, Release It!, 2nd ed. — ch. 4 (stability antipatterns), ch. 5 (stability patterns: circuit breaker, bulkhead, timeout). Extract: cascading-failure prevention. Applied in §7 (I9), §15 (Phase 3).
  • Burns, Designing Distributed Systems — ch. 2 (sidecar), ch. 4 (ambassador), ch. 7 (scatter/gather). Extract: reusable distributed patterns as building blocks. Applied in §9 (ADR-19), §21 (Phase 9).
  • Bass, Weber & Zhu, DevOps: A Software Architect's Perspective — ch. 3 (deployment pipelines), ch. 6 (monitoring). Extract: pipeline-as-architecture, not tooling afterthought. Applied in §29, §22 (Phase 10).
  • Fowler, Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture — ch. 1 (layering), the Unit of Work and Identity Map patterns. Extract: per-service persistence patterns that stay valid even when the "enterprise application" is one service among 27. Applied in §11, §17 (Phase 5).

  • Dean & Barroso, "The Tail at Scale," CACM 2013 — tail-latency amplification across fan-out. Applied in §30, §34.

Reference Implementations

  • Kubernetes — pkg/apis/core/types.go resource conventions. Lesson: declarative desired-state reconciliation. Pitfall: don't copy full CRD-versioning complexity for a 27-service contract registry — over-engineered at this scale. Applied in §10, §29.
  • Istio/Envoy — envoy listener filter chains, xDS discovery protocol. Lesson: sidecar traffic-control patterns (circuit breaking, retries, mTLS). Pitfall: don't adopt full sidecar injection before routing/observability basics are proven. Applied in §9 (ADR-19), §36.
  • Kafka — core/.../kafka/server replication/ISR design; producer idempotence. Lesson: partition-key-driven ordering. Pitfall: don't rely on cross-broker exactly-once transactions across service boundaries. Applied in §9 (ADR-05), §16 (Phase 4).
  • Temporal — service/history workflow state machine; SDK activity retry policy. Lesson: durable workflow execution as an alternative to choreographed sagas. Pitfall: don't adopt it wholesale — this blueprint chooses choreography-first (ADR-09); examined as the road not taken in §36. Applied in §19 (Phase 7), §36.
  • Debezium — debezium-connector-postgres WAL-tailing CDC connector. Lesson: log-based CDC eliminates dual writes. Pitfall: never poll application tables for change. Applied in §9 (ADR-08), §18 (Phase 6).
  • Backstage — packages/catalog-model service-descriptor schema. Lesson: a machine-readable service catalog as the ownership source of truth. Pitfall: don't let the catalog drift from the contract registry. Applied in §11, §10.
  • Envoy's circuit-breaker and outlier-detection config (envoy.extensions.filters.http.router) — Lesson: per-upstream failure thresholds and ejection windows as configuration, not hardcoded constants. Pitfall: don't hardcode the failure threshold and reset timeout that ADR-19 requires stay configurable per downstream. Applied in §15 (Phase 3), §9 (ADR-19).

Glossary (§38) · 54 terms · site-wide glossary

Aggregate
a cluster of domain objects treated as a single consistency unit, owned by exactly one service (P1).
Bounded context
the explicit boundary within which a domain model and its terms have one consistent meaning (Newman).
Database-per-service
the pattern of one datastore owned exclusively by one service, no cross-service table access (P1, ADR-26).
Transactional outbox
a table written in the same database transaction as an aggregate mutation, later relayed to the event backbone (ADR-08).
CDC (Change Data Capture)
tailing a database's write-ahead log to detect committed changes without dual-writing (the Debezium pattern).
At-least-once delivery
a delivery guarantee where a message may be delivered more than once but never zero times (ADR-06).
Idempotency key
a deterministic identifier that lets a consumer detect and collapse duplicate deliveries to one effect (I4).
Dedupe window
the bounded time period during which an idempotency key is remembered to catch redeliveries.
Partition key
the value used to route an event to a specific broker partition, bounding ordering guarantees (I12, ADR-07).
Hot partition
a partition receiving disproportionate traffic due to a poorly chosen partition key (FM-5).
Saga
a sequence of local transactions coordinated across services via events or an orchestrator, each with a compensating action (Garcia-Molina & Salem 1987).
Compensation
the action that semantically undoes a completed saga step when a later step fails.
Choreography
a cross-service workflow coordinated implicitly through events, with no central controller.
Orchestration
a cross-service workflow coordinated explicitly by a central controller that calls each participant.
Schema compatibility mode
the rule (backward/forward/full/none) governing whether a contract change is safe for existing consumers/producers (§8).
Expand/contract migration
a database schema-change strategy that adds before removing, spanning at least two deploys (P11).
Consumer-driven contract
a contract test authored from a consumer's actual usage, run in the producer's CI to prevent breaking changes (P14).
Trace context
the propagated identifiers (trace id, span id, flags) that let a request be reconstructed across services (W3C Trace Context).
Span
a single named, timed operation within a distributed trace.
Correlation id
an identifier tying together every message and log line related to one logical request, distinct from but related to a trace id.
SLO (Service Level Objective)
a target value or range for a measured level of service, e.g. 99.9% availability.
Error budget
the amount of unreliability an SLO permits before a deploy freeze is triggered.
Blast radius
the number of dependent services or users affected when a given component fails.
Distributed monolith
a set of services that are nominally independent but must be deployed together to function correctly (FM-1).
Dual write
writing to two systems (e.g. a database and an event bus) without atomicity, risking divergence between them (ADR-08 rejects it).
Bulkhead
a resource-isolation pattern that prevents one failing dependency from exhausting resources needed by others (Nygard).
Circuit breaker
a stateful guard (closed/open/half-open) that stops calling a failing dependency until it appears to recover (Nygard, I9).
Backpressure
a mechanism by which a slow consumer signals or forces a producer to slow down rather than overflow a buffer (P8).
CloudEvents envelope
the standardized event metadata shape (id, source, type, etc.) this platform's events follow (section 4 of the CloudEvents specification).
Schema registry
a service that stores, versions, and validates contract schemas, enforcing compatibility rules at publish/registration time.
Dead-letter queue (DLQ)
a destination for messages that repeatedly fail processing, removed from the main processing path.
Read-your-writes consistency
a guarantee that a client always sees its own prior writes, even if other clients see them later.
Eventual consistency
a guarantee that, absent new writes, all replicas eventually converge to the same value (Bailis & Ghodsi).
CAP tradeoff
the observation that a distributed system cannot simultaneously guarantee consistency and availability during a network partition (Brewer).
CRDT (Conflict-free Replicated Data Type)
a data structure that can be replicated and merged across nodes without coordination, always converging (Shapiro et al.).
Service mesh
an infrastructure layer, typically sidecar-based, handling service-to-service traffic concerns like retries, mTLS, and observability outside application code.
Canary deployment
a rollout strategy that routes a small percentage of traffic to a new version before a full rollout.
Affected-service detection
the CI mechanism that computes, from a diff, exactly which services' pipelines must run (ADR-20).
Ship checklist
the machine-checkable set of requirements (health check, metrics, tests, contract, CI) every service must satisfy before deployment (§10).
Coordinated omission
a benchmarking error where a load generator waits for a response before issuing the next request, understating tail latency under load.
Tail latency
the latency experienced by the slowest fraction of requests (p99, p999), often dominating perceived performance in fan-out systems (Dean & Barroso).
Chaos engineering
deliberately injecting failure (service kill, network partition, clock skew) into a system to verify it degrades as designed rather than assuming it does.
Jepsen-style testing
a verification methodology (Kingsbury) that checks a distributed system's actual consistency behavior under real network partitions against its documented consistency claims.
Head-based sampling
a tracing-sampling strategy that decides whether to retain a trace at the moment the root span starts, before the outcome of the request is known.
Consumer group
a set of consumer instances that jointly and exclusively consume a topic's partitions, each partition owned by exactly one member at a time.
Optimistic concurrency control
a concurrency strategy that detects write conflicts at commit time (via a version check) rather than locking a resource for the duration of an operation.
Idempotent receiver
a consumer designed so that receiving the same message more than once produces the same effect as receiving it exactly once (the concrete mechanism behind I4).
Fail open / fail closed
a dependency-failure policy choice: fail open degrades functionality but keeps serving (cache-service, Phase 8); fail closed rejects the request outright rather than risk an unsafe state (auth-service, by design).
Read-through cache
a caching pattern where a cache miss triggers the cache itself to populate from the origin, rather than the caller managing the populate step separately.
Single-flight lock
a concurrency guard that collapses many simultaneous requests for the same not-yet-cached key into one origin fetch, preventing a cache stampede (FM-9).
Confused deputy
a security failure in which a component with more privilege than the caller is tricked into misusing that privilege on the caller's behalf (T2).
Golden fixture
a checked-in, version-controlled expected-output file used as the pass/fail reference for a conformance test, so "correct" is a diff against a committed artifact, not a judgment call.
Forward reference (stub)
a consumer handler or integration point built against an event or service contract before the producing service itself exists, closed in a later phase (§17, §21).
Trust boundary
a point in the architecture where the level of authorization or verification changes, requiring explicit authentication/authorization rather than implicit trust (§3).
