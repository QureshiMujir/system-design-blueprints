Hard questions (§34)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. Why is "synchronous replication" in this design not the same as linearizable reads on a standby, and what would have to change to make it so? (§8.9, §11.4, §9 ADR-16)
  2. Derive the quorum-ack threshold formula from first principles and explain why floor(n/2)+1 is chosen over a strict majority of all configured nodes. (§6 P13, §33 #7)
  3. Trace exactly which invariant is violated if a standby advances flushed_lsn before its local fsync completes, and follow the consequence through to WAL truncation on the primary. (§7 I2, I6, §13.6)
  4. Why must the divergence detector walk the full set of timelines recorded in a history file rather than compare only the latest? Name the exact rejoin scenario it would otherwise miss. (§19.2, §19.6)
  5. Explain why DDL is treated as a first-class replicated event rather than an edge case in this design's logical pipeline, and trace the corruption mode of treating it as an edge case instead. (§6 P6, §17, §7 I9/I14)
  6. Compute the disk headroom implied by the default 24-hour slot timeout at the §4 500 MB/s write rate, and explain why a high-write-rate primary tunes this down rather than up. (§4, §33 #4, §15.6)
  7. Why does a controlled handover never produce a timeline fork, while an uncontrolled promotion always risks one? Name the exact structural difference between the two paths. (§18.2, §19.1)
  8. Trace the reason fencing must complete before a promoted replica accepts writes, in terms of the specific invariant it enforces, and name the CI mechanism that verifies it holds. (§7 I15, §18.2, §18.5)
  9. Why does this design report four distinct lag numbers instead of one aggregate figure, and which specific number governs an RPO SLA versus a read-staleness SLA? (§6 P12, §21.1, §4)
  10. Contrast this design's schema-evolution approach against MySQL's row-binlog-as- logical-channel approach — what correctness property does one buy that the other does not guarantee as cleanly? (§17, §32, §35.3)
  11. Derive the cumulative replay lag for a depth-3 cascading chain from the System Constraints table, and explain why cascading lag is additive rather than bounded by the slowest single hop. (§20.3, §33 #10)
  12. Why is the reorder buffer's disk-spill path described as "not optional even in small deployments"? Trace the exact failure class it exists to prevent. (§16.2, §16.6, §23 T6)
  13. What would have to change in this design's failover model to remove the operator-confirmation step entirely, and what does that change cost in terms of the invariants this design currently guarantees structurally? (§18, §35.2, §9 ADR-14)
  14. Why is Amazon Aurora's shared-log architecture a fundamentally different design from this system's cascading-replica read-scaling approach, rather than a parameter tweak on the same architecture? (§32, §35.4, §30)
  15. Trace the exact sequence of events that makes an uncontrolled-failover-then-rejoin scenario forensically recoverable versus silently unrecoverable, naming the specific ordering invariant responsible for the difference. (§7 I10, §18.2, §19.2)
  16. Why does LSN monotonicity (I1) not, by itself, prevent a timeline fork — what additional mechanism is structurally required, and why? (§7 I1 vs I4, §19.1)
  17. Derive the sync-ack latency budget for both same-DC and cross-region deployments, and explain why the cross-region bound is dominated by a single physical constant rather than by anything this design controls. (§33 #6, §4)
  18. Why does this design defer multi-primary conflict resolution (Blueprint #99) rather than build it directly, and which specific invariant would naive multi-writer replication break if added without that separate layer? (§1 non-goals, §6 P1, §32)
  19. Explain the difference between SyncReceive and SyncReplay in terms of which of the four lag numbers (§21.1) each mode waits on, and why one is strictly stronger than the other. (§11.4, §21.1, §8.9)
  20. Why is Kafka's ISR/leader-epoch model cited as the direct ancestor of this design's TimelineId, and what specific truncation problem did KIP-101 solve that invariant I4 generalizes outside a consensus system? (§5 Papers #10, §7 I4, §32)

31. Mental Models & First Principles

  • MM1 — A replica is a fold over an event log, not a copy of a database. The compressed insight: state = fold(apply, initial_state, wal_records); everything about replication is a statement about which prefix of that fold a given consumer has computed. Predicts: any two consumers holding the same prefix hold the same state, regardless of transport. Bug class without it: treating a standby as "a separate database that happens to be kept in sync" leads to ad-hoc reconciliation logic instead of trusting the fold. Applied in §8.9, §13 (Phase 1).
  • MM2 — LSN is a clock, not just an offset. The compressed insight: Lsn totally orders every event the way a Lamport clock orders causally-related events — comparing two LSNs answers "which happened first," full stop. Predicts: any invariant statable as "X must never decrease" (I1, I4) is a clock-monotonicity statement. Bug class without it: comparing LSNs across different timelines as if they were on one clock (exactly the timeline-fork bug, §19). Applied in §7 I1/I4, §19.
  • MM3 — Durability is a race between "acknowledged" and "on disk," and replication adds a second racer. The compressed insight: local fsync (I2) and replication ack (§14) are two independent races to "safe," and a system's actual RPO is whichever racer is slower to cross the threshold the caller asked for. Predicts: any RPO number is meaningless without naming which mode (§11.4) was in effect. Bug class without it: quoting "zero RPO" for a system running in Async mode. Applied in §4, §14.
  • MM4 — A replication slot is a durability promise, not a cursor. The compressed insight: a cursor just remembers a position; a slot additionally obligates the primary to keep serving from that position (I6). Predicts: every "why did WAL grow unboundedly" incident traces to a promise nobody is redeeming. Bug class without it: treating slot cleanup as a garbage-collection nicety rather than an active safety trade-off (P8). Applied in §7 I5/I6, §15.
  • MM5 — A timeline is a version number for "history," not for "data." The compressed insight: the rows don't change meaning across a timeline bump; what changes is which sequence of WAL records is now canonical. Predicts: comparing timelines answers "are we even talking about the same history," a strictly prior question to comparing LSNs. Bug class without it: assuming any two hosts at LSN 100 agree on what happened, without checking they're on the same timeline (§19.6). Applied in §7 I4, §19.
  • MM6 — Logical decoding is a lossy compiler from bytes to intent, and DDL is a change to the target language. The compressed insight: the decoder "compiles" WAL bytes into LogicalChangeEvents using a schema as its type system; a DDL change is a language change mid-compilation. Predicts: any decoder that doesn't version its "language" (schema_version, I14) will eventually misparse. Bug class without it: silent DDL skip (P6). Applied in §17, §7 I9/I14.
  • MM7 — Fencing is mutual exclusion for an entire host, not a lock on a resource. The compressed insight: promotion without fencing is exactly the classic "two processes believe they hold the same lock" bug, just at cluster scale. Predicts: any "auto-failover" design that skips fencing will eventually produce two writers. Bug class without it: assuming a network partition heals cleanly without an explicit exclusion mechanism (P10, I15). Applied in §18, §23 T1.
  • MM8 — Lag is staleness measured against a moving target. The compressed insight: because the primary keeps writing, "how far behind" is a rate comparison (write-rate vs apply-rate), not a static distance — a replica can be "100 MB behind" and either catching up or falling further behind depending on the two rates. Predicts: reporting only bytes (not the rate trend) under-informs an operator. Bug class without it: the "40-minute unnoticed lag" incident, where bytes looked stable-ish while the trend was diverging. Applied in §21, §33 #1/#3.

Required reading (§5)

Books

  • Kleppmann, M. — Designing Data-Intensive Applications, O'Reilly 2017, ch. 5 ("Replication"). Single-leader/multi-leader/leaderless taxonomy; sync/async/ semi-sync trade-offs. Applied in §6 P2, §32 Comparative Architecture, §35 Alternative Architectures.
  • Gray, J. & Reuter, A. — Transaction Processing: Concepts and Techniques, Morgan Kaufmann 1993, ch. 9–11 (logging, locking, recovery). The WAL-discipline vocabulary this protocol assumes from Blueprint #69. Applied in §8 (WAL record layout), §6 P3.
  • Bernstein, P. & Newcomer, E. — Principles of Transaction Processing, 2nd ed., Morgan Kaufmann 2009, ch. 7–8. Replication and recovery, textbook-depth. Applied in §18 (failover), §19 (divergence).
  • Cachin, C., Guerraoui, R., Rodrigues, L. — Reliable and Secure Distributed Programming, 2nd ed., Springer 2011, ch. 5–6 (broadcast, consensus). Theoretical underpinnings for the invariants table. Applied in §7 (System Invariants), §35.2.
  • Weikum, G. & Vossen, G. — Transactional Information Systems, Morgan Kaufmann 2001, ch. 11 (redo/undo recovery, media recovery). Cross-checks the ARIES-adjacent recovery assumptions this protocol's apply path depends on. Applied in §13 (apply path).
  • Tanenbaum, A. & Van Steen, M. — Distributed Systems: Principles and Paradigms, 3rd ed., ch. 7–8 (consistency, replication). General replication taxonomy grounding the Mental Models (§31). Applied in §31, §32.

Papers

  • Mohan, C., Haderle, D., Lindsay, B., Pirahesh, H., Schwarz, P. — "ARIES: A Transaction Recovery Method..." ACM TODS, March 1992. The LSN abstraction and force-log-before-data discipline. Applied in §7 I2/I3, §8, §11.1.
  • van Renesse, R. & Schneider, F. — "Chain Replication for Supporting High Throughput and Availability." OSDI 2004. Applied in §32 Comparative Architecture, §35.2.
  • DeCandia, G. et al. — "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store." SOSP 2007. Quorum read/write trade-off; sloppy quorum + hinted handoff contrast. Applied in §6 P13, §33 #7.
  • Oki, B. & Liskov, B. — "Viewstamped Replication: A New Primary Copy Method to Support Highly-Available Distributed Systems." PODC 1988. View-change-as-failover precedent. Applied in §18 (failover state machine), §35.2.
  • Ongaro, D. & Ousterhout, J. — "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm (Raft)." USENIX ATC 2014. Leader-term-as-timeline analogy. Applied in §7 I4, §19, §35.2.
  • Gray, J., Helland, P., O'Neil, P., Shasha, D. — "The Dangers of Replication and a Solution." SIGMOD 1996. Why naive eager multi-primary replication doesn't scale; motivates ADR-14's single-writer default. Applied in §6 P1, §30.
  • Verbitski, A. et al. — "Amazon Aurora: Design Considerations for High Throughput Cloud-Native Relational Databases." SIGMOD 2017. Log-is-the-database architecture. Applied in §32, §35.4.
  • Corbett, J. et al. — "Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database." OSDI 2012. Paxos-replicated tablets, TrueTime. Applied in §30 (scalability), §35.2.
  • Helland, P. & Campbell, D. — "Building on Quicksand." CIDR 2009. Log shipping is insufficient for cross-region consistency without a divergence story. Applied in §19 (Phase 7), §23 T1.
  • Kafka KIP-101 — "Alter Replication Protocol to use Leader Epoch rather than High Watermark for Truncation." 2017. The canonical timeline-fork-detection treatment. Applied in §7 I4, §19 (Phase 7, primary reference).

Reference Implementations

  • PostgreSQL — src/backend/replication/ (walsender.c, walreceiver.c, logical/, slot.c). Study the slot-advance atomic-rename pattern; do not copy the C memory-lifetime idioms. Applied in §13, §15, §16.
  • MySQL — sql/rpl_* (rpl_replica.cc, rpl_source.cc, rpl_gtid_*). Row vs statement vs mixed binlog; GTID scheme contrasted with our LSN scheme. Applied in §17 (schema/DDL), §35.3.
  • etcd/raft (github.com/etcd-io/raft). Reference for the leader-epoch/term bookkeeping this protocol's TimelineId generalizes outside consensus. Applied in §19, §35.2.
  • WAL-G (github.com/wal-g/wal-g). Reference for WAL-archive-based PITR bootstrap sharing a slot's restart_lsn. Applied in §13.3 (PhaseDeferred), §26.
  • Debezium — debezium-connector-postgres/, debezium-connector-mysql/. The productionized logical-decoding consumer side: snapshot-then-stream, schema-history topic. Applied in §16, §17, §26.

Glossary (§37) · 60 terms · site-wide glossary

LSN (Lsn)
64-bit monotonic byte offset into a timeline's WAL; the system's global ordering primitive (I1).
TimelineId
4-byte monotonic identifier bumped on every promotion; the fork-detectability primitive (I4, P5).
WAL (Write-Ahead Log)
the append-only durability log every replication consumer reads from, format owned by Blueprint #69 (ADR-03).
walsender
the primary-side (or cascading-standby-side) process/task streaming WAL to a consumer.
walreceiver
the standby-side task receiving and persisting streamed WAL.
Replication slot
the durability contract preventing WAL truncation ahead of a registered consumer (P8, I5, I6).
restart_lsn
the LSN before which a primary will not truncate WAL for a given slot.
confirmed_flush_lsn
the LSN a slot's consumer has durably confirmed receipt of.
catalog_xmin
for logical slots, the oldest catalog snapshot the decoder still needs.
XLogRecord
one physical WAL record (§8.1 layout).
XLogData
the wire message carrying one or more XLogRecords.
KeepAlive
a walsender heartbeat frame, optionally requesting a status reply.
StandbyStatusUpdate
the four-LSN status frame a walreceiver sends upstream.
HotStandbyFeedback
a walreceiver frame informing the primary of the standby's oldest still-needed transaction horizon.
Reorder buffer
the logical-decode component grouping row operations by transaction ID, emitting in commit order (I3, I8).
Output plugin
the pluggable serializer converting LogicalChangeEvents to a wire format (Avro/JSON/protobuf, ADR-08).
Logical decoding
converting physical WAL records into row/statement-level change events.
Physical replication
byte-for-byte WAL shipping and replay.
Synchronous replication
a mode where the primary waits for at least one standby's acknowledgement before completing a commit (P2).
Asynchronous replication
the default mode; the primary never waits for replication acknowledgement (ADR-04).
Quorum replication
waiting for N of M standbys to acknowledge, threshold floor(n/2)+1 by default (P13, ADR-06).
Semi-synchronous replication
the general industry term for what this design splits into SyncReceive/SyncReplay (§11.4).
Cascading replica
a standby whose WAL source is another standby, not the primary (§20).
Controlled handover
a planned, zero-data-loss failover with all hosts converging on one LSN before the timeline bump (§18.2).
Uncontrolled promotion
an unplanned failover with a bounded-loss fork risk, requiring fencing first (§18.2, I15).
Fencing
the act of preventing an old primary from accepting writes after a promotion (STONITH/quorum/operator, §18.4).
STONITH
"Shoot The Other Node In The Head"; a hardware-level fencing mechanism.
Divergence
the state where two hosts' histories have forked and cannot be reconciled without data loss (§19).
Timeline fork
the point at which two timelines share a prefix but diverge (§19.1).
History file
the per-host append-only record of timeline boundaries and fork LSNs (§11.2).
Fork LSN
the LSN at which a timeline fork occurred.
Rebase / rewind
reconciling a divergent host onto the current timeline after operator acknowledgement (§19.3–19.4).
RPO (Recovery Point Objective)
the maximum acceptable committed-data loss, measured here in bytes bounded by flush_lag (async) or zero (quorum, §4).
RTO (Recovery Time Objective)
the maximum acceptable time to restore write availability after a fault (§4, M7/M8).
Replay lag
primary.flush_lsn − standby.replay_lsn; the read-staleness number (§21.1).
Flush lag
primary.flush_lsn − standby.flushed_lsn; the RPO-exposure number.
Receive lag
primary.flush_lsn − standby.received_lsn; the network-transit number.
Send lag
primary.flush_lsn − walsender.last_sent_lsn; the primary-side send-queue number.
Publication
the named configuration governing which tables and DDL-policy a logical slot exposes (§17.2).
DDL policy
Deny/AllowAdditive/AllowAll; governs how schema changes flow to logical consumers (ADR-09, I9).
Schema version
the monotonic per-table version every LogicalChangeEvent carries (I14).
GTID (Global Transaction Identifier)
MySQL's transaction-identity scheme, contrasted with this design's LSN scheme (§5 Ref-impl #2).
Binlog
MySQL's WAL-equivalent, in statement/row/mixed formats (§35.3).
CDC (Change Data Capture)
the general term for logical-replication-driven downstream consumption (Kreps 2014, §5).
Avro
the default logical output-plugin wire format, schema-carrying (ADR-08).
Snapshot (logical)
a new logical slot's consistent initial table state, streamed before change events (§16.2).
PITR (Point-in-Time Recovery)
replaying archived WAL to a specific LSN/time, sharing this design's slot restart_lsn as anchor (PhaseDeferred("§13.3")).
Checkpoint
the periodic point at which the primary consults min_restart_lsn() for WAL-truncation eligibility (§15.2).
ARIES
the WAL/recovery discipline (Mohan et al. 1992) this design's durability ordering (I2, I3) is built on top of.
Backpressure
the flow-control response to a slow consumer (rate-limiting DDL floods, §17.6; sync-ack timeout, §14.2).
mTLS
mutual TLS; the mandatory cross-DC transport-security mode (ADR-12).
CRC32C
the per-frame integrity check gating watermark advancement (I7).
Framing
the length-prefixed, CRC-trailed on-wire message boundary discipline (ADR-11, §8.2).
Batching
packing multiple WAL records into one XLogData frame to amortize codec/syscall overhead (§22.2).
TCP_NODELAY
the per-slot socket flag trading coalescing efficiency against latency (§22.2).
Split-brain
two hosts simultaneously believing themselves primary and accepting writes (T1, FM2).
Leader epoch
Kafka's KIP-101 term for this design's TimelineId (§5 Papers #10).
High watermark
the pre-KIP-101 Kafka truncation-safety mechanism that leader epochs replaced; this design's I4/history-file model is the direct descendant.
ISR (In-Sync Replica set)
Kafka's quorum-membership concept, analogous to this design's "eligible sync-candidate set" (§14.2, §32).
Log-is-the-database
the Aurora architectural pattern where replicas read a shared log directly instead of applying WAL locally (§32, §35.4).
