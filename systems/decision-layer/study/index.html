Hard questions (§35)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. Why does anchor resolution use a strict precedence order (P4) instead of a confidence-weighted blend across tiers, and what breaks without it? (§6 P4, §7 I5)
  2. How does I3's replay-reproduces-graph guarantee survive a tree-sitter grammar version bump mid-history? (§7 I3, §27, §18)
  3. Why is drift detection structurally forbidden from auto-resolving (P2), even for an apparently obvious fix like a pure rename — and how does that connect to Cunningham's technical-debt framing? (§6 P2, §7 I6, §5 Ward Cunningham)
  4. What guarantees two team-backend replicas converge to the same graph regardless of op-arrival order, and why does that require a logical clock rather than a synchronized wall clock? (§6 P12, §7 I9/I10, §9 ADR-22)
  5. Why does the backend shard by repo_id instead of needing a distributed graph database, and what would force a redesign of that assumption? (§31, §9 ADR-07)
  6. How does compliance export achieve independent verifiability without either party trusting the other, and which specific fields make forgery detectable? (§6 P11, §7 I14, §24 T7/T8)
  7. Trace what happens when a developer renames a function and moves it to a different file in the same commit — which anchor tiers fire, in what order, and which DriftKind results? (§8, §14, §17)
  8. Why is dlcore compiled to WASM for the extension instead of a native Node addon, and under what deployment scenario would that decision be wrong? (§9 ADR-04, §1)
  9. What is the actual bottleneck at the system's practical scale ceiling, and why is it drift-scan wall clock rather than index memory or sync bandwidth? (§31, §34)
  10. Why does anchor-repair bound its AST-diff search window (ADR-19) instead of running unbounded tree-edit distance, and what real system's failure mode motivates the bound? (§8, §5 GumTree, §9 ADR-19)
  11. How would a malicious repo attempt to exploit the tree-sitter parsing layer, and what two independent structural defenses stop it from taking down the whole index? (§24 T6, §5)
  12. Why does the system treat a repo-global decision as an explicit scope tag rather than an anchor list that happens to be empty? (§6 P5, §7 I1)
  13. What would silently break if the op-log's hash chain were removed, and which two invariants specifically depend on it? (§7 I3/I4, §6 P3)
  14. Why does forking use copy-on-read against a frozen ReplayCursor rather than copy-on-write, and what isolation bug does that avoid? (§20, §11)
  15. How does Decision Layer's staleness-detection model differ fundamentally from Swimm's, and what does that difference cost in operational overhead? (§33, §6 P1)
  16. Why is telemetry redaction a mandatory pipeline stage rather than an opt-out default, and which principle and threat does that jointly satisfy? (§6 P13, §24 T5)
  17. What is the concrete, numeric argument for why 20M LOC / 200,000 files was chosen as the top-line scale ceiling rather than an order of magnitude higher or lower? (§4, §34)
  18. If the team needed to support a language with no maintained tree-sitter grammar, what would have to change, and why was that explicitly deferred rather than designed in from Phase 1? (§37, §21, §36)
  19. Pick ADR-07 (Postgres over Neo4j) and describe the concrete regime where the rejected choice becomes correct — why does that column matter operationally, not just academically? (§9, §31)
  20. What is the minimal sequence of failures that would let a decision-log tampering attack succeed undetected, and which invariant closes that gap? (§24 T3/T7, §7 I3/I4)

32. Mental Models & First Principles

MM1 — An anchor is a hypothesis about identity, and every edit is an experiment that can falsify it. Predicts: any edit can invalidate an anchor at any precedence tier, so resolution must always re-verify, never trust a cache. Bug class without it: caching a resolved anchor across an edit event and silently serving a stale binding. Resolves in: P1 (§6), I2 (§7).

MM2 — Precedence order is a total order, not a voting system. Predicts: two candidate matches are never blended or scored against each other outside the fixed tier order. Bug class without it: nondeterministic resolution when a lower-tier match happens to look more confident than a higher-tier one. Resolves in: P4 (§6), I5 (§7).

MM3 — Append-only history turns "what happened" into a pure function of the log, never of memory. Predicts: any two processes folding the same log converge to the same state, with no reliance on runtime side-effect ordering. Bug class without it: a decision graph silently diverging between two machines with no way to detect it. Resolves in: P3 (§6), I3 (§7), Phase 6 (§18).

MM4 — Drift is a detector's opinion, not an autopilot's correction. Predicts: the system's job stops at classification; resolution is always a human action with an audit trail. Bug class without it: an auto-"fix" that silently erases the original decision's intent, making history untrustworthy. Resolves in: P2 (§6), I6 (§7).

MM5 — A decision graph's edges are a controlled vocabulary, not free text. Predicts: every relationship between decisions is one of exactly three typed kinds, enabling typed traversal queries instead of text search. Bug class without it: an unqueryable pile of prose cross-references that can't answer "what depends on X" at scale. Resolves in: P10 (§6), Phase 8 (§20).

MM6 — Logical time, not wall-clock time, is what makes merges deterministic. Predicts: two replicas receiving the same ops in different physical arrival order still converge, because ordering is a property of the Lamport clock plus actor-id tie-break, not of arrival time. Bug class without it: split-brain graphs that "usually" converge but occasionally don't, reproducing only under specific race conditions. Resolves in: P12 (§6), I9/I10 (§7), Phase 7 (§19).

MM7 — A compliance export is only as trustworthy as its ability to be independently re-derived. Predicts: an auditor who does not trust the exporting team can still verify the bundle by replaying the same cursor and comparing hashes. Bug class without it: an export format that is internally consistent but unfalsifiable by an outside party, making "compliant" a matter of trust rather than proof. Resolves in: P11 (§6), I14 (§7), Phase 10 (§22).

MM8 — Incremental computation is a memoization discipline, not a performance afterthought. Predicts: the system's entire interactive-latency budget (§4) depends on never recomputing what a revision-stamped input hasn't invalidated, architected in from Phase 1, not bolted on later. Bug class without it: a system fast on first index and unusably slow by the hundredth keystroke because every event triggers full recomputation. Resolves in: P8 (§6), ADR-08 (§9), Phase 1 (§13).

5. Required Reading

Books

  • Crafting Interpreters, Robert Nystrom — Ch. 5 ("Representing Code") and Ch. 6 ("Parsing Expressions"): extract AST node design and the visitor-pattern traversal shape reused by ast-indexer. Applied in §11.

  • Engineering a Compiler (2nd ed.), Cooper & Torczon — Ch. 3–4 (scanning, syntax analysis): extract parse-tree-vs-AST tradeoffs and error-recovery strategy for malformed input. Applied in Phase 1 (§13).

  • Designing Data-Intensive Applications, Martin Kleppmann — Ch. 5 (Replication), Ch. 7 (Transactions), Ch. 11 (Stream Processing): extract op-log replication and conflict semantics reused by op-log merge (§8) and Phase 7 sync. Applied in §8 and Phase 7 (§18).

  • Database Internals, Alex Petrov — Ch. on write-ahead logs and LSM storage: extract append-only, content-addressed storage design reused by decision-store. Applied in Phase 6 (§17).

  • A Philosophy of Software Design, John Ousterhout — chapters on complexity and information hiding: extract the framing of a decision as an information-hiding boundary worth recording. Applied in §6 (P1, P3).

  • Working Effectively with Legacy Code, Michael Feathers — chapters on seams and characterization tests: extract the notion of a "seam" as a structural analog to an anchor — a point where behavior can be pinned down and later verified. Applied in Phase 4 (§16) and Phase 5 (§17).

  • Site Reliability Engineering, Google (Beyer, Jones, Petoff, Murphy) — chapters on SLOs and incident response: extract the operational-readiness template reused in the Operational Readiness section. Applied in Operational Readiness (§26).

Papers

  • Michael Nygard, 2011, "Documenting Architecture Decisions" — origin of the ADR format; contribution: decisions as short, immutable, numbered records. Applied in §1 and §8 (decision record schema).

  • D.L. Parnas, P.C. Clements, 1986, "A Rational Design Process: How and Why to Fake It", IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering — contribution: real design is not rational but must be documented as if it were, retroactively. Applied in §1 and §6 (P1).

  • Donald Knuth, 1984, "Literate Programming", The Computer Journal — contribution: code and its rationale belong in one artifact, ordered for human understanding, not machine execution order. Applied in §2 and Phase 3 (§14, capture UI ordering).

  • Eugene W. Myers, 1986, "An O(ND) Difference Algorithm and Its Variations", Algorithmica — contribution: the O(ND) diff algorithm underlying line-level change detection used to scope anchor-repair search (§8). Applied in Phase 4 (§16).

  • Jean-Rémy Falleri, Floréal Morandat, Éric Blanc, Matias Martinez, Martin Monperrus, 2014, "Fine-grained and Accurate Source Code Differencing" (GumTree), ASE 2014 — contribution: bottom-up/top-down AST node matching (tree edit distance) for tracking node identity across versions. Applied in §8 (AST path resolution) and Phase 2 (§13).

  • Jonathan Sillito, Gail C. Murphy, Kris De Volder, 2006, "Questions Programmers Ask During Software Evolution Tasks", FSE 2006 — contribution: catalog of the questions developers actually ask when changing code, motivating what a decision record must answer. Applied in §1 and §8 (decision record schema fields).

  • Thomas D. LaToza, Brad A. Myers, 2010, "Hard-to-Answer Questions About Code" — contribution: identifies "why" questions as the hardest and least-supported by existing tools. Applied in §2 (why build this) and Phase 3 (§14).

  • M.M. Lehman, 1980, "Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution", Proceedings of the IEEE — contribution: software must continually change or become progressively less useful (law of continuing change); motivates drift detection as an ongoing, never-finished process. Applied in §2 and Phase 5 (§17).

  • Ward Cunningham, 1992, "The WyCash Portfolio Management System" (OOPSLA experience report) — contribution: origin of the technical-debt metaphor; a decision knowingly deferred is debt that must be tracked. Applied in §8 (decision lifecycle) and §6 (P7).

Reference Implementations

  • tree-sitter (github.com/tree-sitter/tree-sitter), Max Brunsfeld — study lib/src/parser.c (incremental parse core) and lib/src/tree.c (tree structure, byte-range tracking). Lesson: incremental reparse via edit-scoped tree patching, not full retokenization. Pitfall: external scanners are hand-written state machines that silently diverge across grammar versions — pin grammar versions explicitly (ADR-14). Applied in Phase 1 (§13).

  • adr-tools (github.com/npryce/adr-tools) — study src/adr-new and templates/template.md. Lesson: markdown-first, sequentially numbered, immutable decision records. Pitfall: no binding to code and no drift detection — purely a convention, which is why Decision Layer adds anchors. Applied in §8 (decision record schema) and Comparative Architecture (§33).

  • GumTree (github.com/GumTreeDiff/gumtree) — study core/src/main/java/com/github/gumtreediff/matchers/heuristic (bottom-up/top-down matchers). Lesson: two-phase node matching (isomorphic subtree anchoring, then height- bounded container matching) for tree edit distance. Pitfall: quadratic worst case on large subtree moves — bound the search window (ADR-19). Applied in Phase 2 (§13) and Phase 8 (§20, AST-path repair).

  • Sourcegraph SCIP (github.com/sourcegraph/scip) — study scip.proto (symbol scheme) and bindings/typescript. Lesson: symbol identity encoded as package+descriptor path, decoupled from file position. Pitfall: index goes stale silently if not regenerated per commit — tie regeneration to the same file-watcher trigger as anchor repair (I2). Applied in §8 (symbol_ref field) and Phase 9 (§21).

  • rust-analyzer salsa (github.com/salsa-rs/salsa) — study src/derived.rs (memoized query storage) and src/runtime.rs (revision counters). Lesson: incremental recomputation via input revision stamps and memoized query dependency tracking. Pitfall: coarse query granularity causes over-invalidation cascades — scope queries per-file, not per-workspace (ADR-08). Applied in Phase 1 (§13) and Phase 4 (§16).

  • difftastic (github.com/Wilfred/difftastic) — study src/parse/tree_sitter_parser.rs and src/diff/changes.rs. Lesson: syntax-aware diff using AST alignment rather than line alignment. Pitfall: performance cliff on deeply nested syntax lacking distinguishing anchor tokens — cap tree-diff depth and fall back to content hash (§8). Applied in Phase 5 (§17, drift classification).

RFCs / Specifications

  • Language Server Protocol Specification (current version), Microsoft — section 3.4 (document synchronization) and section 3.17 (custom requests). Applied in Phase 3 (§14) and §3 (lsp-bridge).

  • SCIP protobuf schema and LSIF (Language Server Index Format), Sourcegraph/Microsoft — symbol and occurrence encoding. Applied in Phase 9 (§21).

  • JSON Schema (2020-12 draft) — used to validate Decision and ExportBundle serialized forms. Applied in §11 and Phase 10 (§22).

  • Semantic Versioning 2.0.0 — grammar/parser version pinning policy. Applied in Compatibility & Versioning (§27).

  • SPDX Specification (current release) and CycloneDX Specification — compliance bundle shape for compliance-exporter. Applied in Phase 10 (§22).

Glossary (§38) · 54 terms · site-wide glossary

Anchor
the multi-tier binding (symbol ref, AST path, content hash, fuzzy window) tying a decision to concrete code (§8, §11).
Anchor repair
the six-step procedure re-resolving an anchor after an invalidating edit, walking the precedence tiers in order (§8).
Orphaned anchor
an anchor for which no tier resolved a candidate; flagged, never silently dropped (I2).
AST path
a root-relative sequence of node-type + child-index steps identifying a position in a syntax tree independent of byte offset (§11 AstPath).
Symbol identity
binding by qualified name + kind rather than by position; the highest anchor precedence tier (§8).
Content hash
a blake3 digest (ADR-06) of an anchor's enclosing block, whitespace- normalized; third anchor precedence tier.
Drift
detected divergence between a recorded decision and current code state, classified into the five-way DriftKind taxonomy (§8).
Drift taxonomy
Moved, Renamed, ModifiedInPlace, Deleted, SemanticallyContradicted (§11 DriftKind).
Decision graph
the full set of Decision records and their typed DecisionEdges, reconstructed by replay (§8, §11).
Supersedes edge
a typed DecisionEdge marking one decision as replacing another, retiring, never deleting, the prior record (P7).
Replay cursor
(repo_id, op_offset, graph_hash) marking a specific fold position in the op-log; the unit compliance exports are scoped and re-derived by (§11 ReplayCursor).
Incremental reparse
re-parsing only the edited byte range of a file rather than the whole file, bounding reparse latency (P8, ADR-08).
Salsa-style memoization
revision-stamped, dependency-tracked query caching (rust-analyzer salsa, §5) underlying dlcore's incremental computation model (ADR-08).
SCIP
Sourcegraph's symbol/occurrence index format; source of the qualified-name scheme Decision Layer's symbol_ref encoding borrows (§5, §21).
Compliance bundle
the ExportBundle produced by compliance-exporter: decisions, provenance, replay cursor, bundle hash — independently re-derivable (P11, §11).
Fuzzy window
the lowest-precedence anchor tier: a text window matched under an edit-distance threshold, last resort before orphaning (§8).
Precedence tier
one of the four ordered anchor-resolution levels (symbol ref → AST path → content hash → fuzzy window), never voted or blended (P4).
Assertion rule
a structured, AST-pattern-based rule attached to a decision that the drift detector evaluates against the resolved anchor's current subtree (§8, §17).
Decision scope
Anchored or RepoGlobal; every decision must declare one explicitly (P5, I1).
Op-log
the append-only, content-hash-chained, length-prefixed sequence of records that is the sole writer of decision state (P3, §18).
Checkpoint
a periodic snapshot of the folded decision graph plus its ReplayCursor, bounding cold-start replay time (§18).
Lamport timestamp
a logical clock value used, with actor id, to totally order op-log entries across writers independent of wall-clock skew (ADR-22, P12).
Actor id
the identifier of the writer (developer or process) that produced an op-log entry or SyncOp, required on every op (I9).
Logical clock
the Lamport-timestamp-plus-actor-id ordering scheme underlying deterministic multi-writer merge (P12).
SyncOp
the wire unit sync-client sends to team-backend: op type, logical clock, actor id, payload (§11).
Tenant isolation
the guarantee that no query or write crosses tenant_id boundaries in team-backend (T4, I8).
Fork
a logical repo_id whose op-log begins as a copy-on-read view of a base graph up to a frozen ReplayCursor, isolated until an explicit merge-back (§20).
Merge-back
replaying a fork's op-log tail against the base's current tip using the same deterministic merge logic as team sync (§20, P12).
Blast-radius query
a bounded transitive-closure traversal finding all decisions a given anchor's retirement would affect via depends-on chains (§20).
Typed traversal query
a graph query over the three fixed EdgeKinds, never free-text search (P10, §20).
Edge kind
Supersedes, DependsOn, Contradicts; the closed set of decision-graph relationship types (§11 EdgeKind).
DriftKind
the closed five-variant enum classifying a drift event (§11).
AnchorKind
the closed enum tagging which precedence tier an anchor last resolved at (§11).
Content-addressed storage
storing/identifying data by the hash of its content rather than a mutable location, underlying both the op-log and object storage (§9 ADR-05/ADR-11).
Hash chain
each op-log record's hash including the prior record's hash, making tampering structurally detectable (I4, §24 T3).
Tree edit distance
the minimum-cost sequence of node operations transforming one AST into another; the theoretical basis for AST-path matching (Falleri et al. 2014, §5).
GumTree matching
the bottom-up/top-down bounded node-matching heuristic Decision Layer's AST-path resolver adapts (ADR-19, §5).
External scanner
a tree-sitter grammar's hand-written scanner for context-sensitive tokens, a source of cross-grammar-version divergence if unpinned (§5, ADR-14).
Grammar version lockfile
the explicit per-language tree-sitter commit-hash pin preventing silent AST-shape drift (ADR-14).
WASM core
dlcore compiled to WebAssembly for portable execution inside the VS Code extension host (ADR-04).
LSP custom method
a decisionLayer/* extension to the Language Server Protocol exposing dlcore operations to any LSP-aware editor (ADR-03, §5).
Backpressure
the bounded-queue-with-backoff discipline preventing unbounded memory growth during backend outages (ADR-23, FM-4).
Bounded queue
the fixed-capacity local queue sync-client uses to hold undelivered SyncOps during an outage (ADR-23).
Canary release
promoting a new team-backend build to a small tenant fraction before a full rolling upgrade (§26).
Rolling upgrade
replacing stateless API-gateway replicas one at a time with zero downtime (§26).
SBOM
Software Bill of Materials; the per-release dependency manifest feeding both release provenance and the compliance-exporter's own audit shape (§29).
Provenance attestation
a SLSA-style signed record of a release artifact's build platform, source commit, and build steps (§29).
SLO
Service Level Objective; the derived latency/availability targets in §26 tracing directly back to §4's constraints.
RTO / RPO
Recovery Time Objective / Recovery Point Objective; the ≤ 30 min / ≤ 5 min team-backend restore targets (§4, §26).
Conformance fixture
a named, permanent input/expected-output pair under conformance/fixtures/, the executable form of this specification (§10, §28).
Differential testing
cross-checking classification output against an independent oracle (git log --follow, GumTree) rather than a hand-written expected value (§28).
Chaos testing
injected infrastructure faults (Postgres failover, network partition, clock skew) verifying invariants hold under failure, not just the happy path (§28).
Soak testing
sustained multi-day execution catching slow leaks and queue-depth creep invisible in short test runs (§28).
Fault injection
deliberately corrupting or truncating inputs (bit-flip, partial write, oversized payload) to verify the system's designed failure path, not just its success path (§28).
