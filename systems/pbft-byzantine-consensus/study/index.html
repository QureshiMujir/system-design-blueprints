Hard questions (§33)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. Why does prepared(m,v,n) require 2f matching prepares excluding the primary's own, and what breaks if the primary's implicit prepare is double-counted? (§7 I3; Phase 13 §13.6)
  2. Why is the client's acceptance quorum f+1 while every internal protocol quorum is 2f+1, and why would using 2f+1 at the client be wasteful rather than merely different? (§7 I9; §32 #2)
  3. Trace what happens if a view change's O-set construction used the lowest-view P-set entry instead of the highest-view one — which invariant breaks and why? (Phase 15 §15.2; §7 I6)
  4. Why does the MAC authenticator backend require a different O-set digest-selection rule (plurality) than the transferable backends, and what does "transferable" formally buy you at that decision point? (Phase 14 §14.6; Phase 15 §15.2, §15.5)
  5. Derive why K ≪ L is required for the checkpoint/pipeline relationship to avoid stalling — what happens at the boundary K = L? (Phase 16 §16.6; §4 constraints table)
  6. Why does state transfer accept f+1 matching responses but checkpoints require 2f+1 matching messages to become stable — reconcile the two thresholds via quorum-intersection. (Phase 17 §17.6; §7 I7)
  7. Explain why HotStuff's three-chain commit rule cannot be safely relaxed to two chains, tying the argument to the "locking rule" it depends on. (Phase 18 §18.6; MM7 §30)
  8. Why is BLS proof-of-possession mandatory rather than optional hardening, and what specific attack does omitting it enable? (Phase 14 §14.6; §22 T9; §7 I13)
  9. Why does this design forbid probabilistic safety even while permitting a deferred asynchronous variant with probabilistic liveness — what is the formal distinction? (§6 P9; §35 Deferred Features Register)
  10. Why must the view-change timer read a monotonic clock instead of wall-clock time, and what concrete attack does a wall-clock timer enable? (§6 P11; §22 T11)
  11. Reconcile the 3f+1 lower bound (I2) with a hypothetical 3f-replica deployment "in honest-majority settings" — what specific safety violation, not just a liveness one, results? (§6 P2; §32 #2, #3)
  12. Why does batching (B=256) require per-batch atomicity, and what specific invariant does partial-batch execution violate? (Phase 21 §21.6; §7 I12)
  13. Explain why parallel signature verification requires post-verification reordering by (view,sequence) — what would silently break without it? (Phase 21 §21.2, §21.6; §7 I12)
  14. Why does reconfiguration require |R_old ∩ R_new| ≥ max(f_old,f_new)+1, and what split-brain scenario does violating this enable across the epoch boundary? (Phase 20 §20.2; §7 I14)
  15. Compare the message-complexity Big-O of bft-core's view-change against bft-hotstuff's and derive the crossover n at which the linear core becomes favorable, using §32's formulas. (§29; §32 #7, #8)
  16. Why is Zyzzyva's speculative execution unsuitable as this design's default path even though it is faster in the common case — what does it trade away? (§31 Comparative Architecture Table; §6 P1)
  17. Trace a state-transfer request racing a fresh checkpoint that advances past the requested target — what does the recovering replica do, and which invariant guarantees it terminates? (Phase 17 §17.8; §7 I7, I8)
  18. Why does this blueprint reject Nakamoto-style probabilistic finality even for the deferred stretch goals, and what problem is Nakamoto consensus solving that this design's mandate excludes by construction? (§34 Alternative Architectures; §1 Goal & Scope)

30. Mental Models & First Principles

  1. MM1 — A Byzantine replica is an adversary with the whole protocol spec, not a slower correct replica. Predicts: any code path assuming "worst case is a crash" has a latent safety hole. Bug without it: treating message-absence and message-forgery as the same failure mode. Resolves in: §7 (I1) / Phase 13.
  2. MM2 — prepared/committed-local are predicates over the log, not events you received. Predicts: WAL replay after a crash must reconstruct identical predicate values to the live path. Bug without it: divergence between "replay" and "live" produces different safety state. Resolves in: §7 (I3, I4) / §11.4.
  3. MM3 — The view-change P-set is a proof-carrying handoff, not a vote. Predicts: a new primary ignoring the P-set commits nothing safely — it is what makes a view change a continuation, not a restart. Bug without it: "just start fresh on view-change" silently violates safety across the epoch boundary. Resolves in: Phase 15 / I5, I6.
  4. MM4 — Quorum size 2f+1 exists so any two quorums intersect in ≥1 correct replica; every other design choice in PBFT is a consequence of that one arithmetic fact. Predicts: any internal threshold below 2f+1 (except the client's f+1, I9) is a bug. Bug without it: implementers use f+1 internally by false analogy to the client path. Resolves in: §7 (I2) / §32 Back-of-Envelope Capacity Model.
  5. MM5 — Authentication and consensus are orthogonal axes. Predicts: swapping MAC for BLS changes performance and evidence-transferability, never the safety argument. Bug without it: coupling view-change logic to a specific authenticator breaks transferability assumptions silently when the backend changes. Resolves in: Phase 14 / Phase 15 §15.5 / P5.
  6. MM6 — A timer is a liveness knob, never a safety knob. Predicts: changing T_base alone can never fix a safety bug, only a liveness one; if tuning a timer "fixes" equivocation, the real bug is elsewhere. Bug without it: teams tune timers to paper over predicate bugs. Resolves in: §6 (P1, P11) / Phase 15.
  7. MM7 — HotStuff's three-chain commit rule is PBFT's three phases unrolled across block heights, not less cryptographic work. Predicts: the linear pacemaker's O(n) cost comes from restructuring the quadratic all-to-all exchange into a Qc aggregate, not from doing less work overall. Bug without it: assuming HotStuff is "cheaper crypto" rather than "same crypto, restructured." Resolves in: Phase 18 / §31 Comparative Architecture Table.
  8. MM8 — Garbage collection is a liveness optimization riding on a safety precondition. Predicts: any GC path firing before the 2f+1-checkpoint precondition (I7) is a latent safety bug disguised as storage hygiene. Bug without it: engineers skip the quorum check under storage-pressure incidents, treating checkpoint/GC as "just disk cleanup." Resolves in: §7 (I7, I8) / Phase 16.

Required reading (§5)

Books

  • Cachin, C.; Guerraoui, R.; Rodrigues, L. — Introduction to Reliable and Secure Distributed Programming, 2nd ed., Springer, 2011. Ch. 3 (Reliable Broadcast) and Ch. 5 (Consensus), including the Byzantine variants. Extract: the abstraction hierarchy from reliable broadcast to consensus that Bracha's echo pattern (below) instantiates. Applied in §8 Domain Model and Phase 13.
  • Lynch, N. A. — Distributed Algorithms, Morgan Kaufmann, 1996. Ch. 6 (Byzantine Agreement) and Ch. 24 (Partially Synchronous Models). Extract: the formal proof machinery behind the 3f+1 bound and the partial-synchrony liveness argument. Applied in §12 Phase 0 (boot-time n≥3f+1 check) and Phase 15 (view-change timer).
  • Attiya, H.; Welch, J. — Distributed Computing: Fundamentals, Simulations, and Advanced Topics, 2nd ed., Wiley, 2004. Ch. 5 (Fault-Tolerant Consensus), the Byzantine lower-bound proofs. Extract: the impossibility-with-3f-replicas argument in full formal detail. Applied in §7 Invariants (I2).
  • Ghosh, S. — Distributed Systems: An Algorithmic Approach, 2nd ed., CRC Press, 2014. Ch. 14 (Fault Tolerance), Byzantine agreement section. Extract: the oral-message vs. signed-message algorithm distinction, the ancestor of this design's MAC-vs-signature ADR-05. Applied in Phase 14.
  • Kleppmann, M. — Designing Data-Intensive Applications, O'Reilly, 2017. Ch. 9 (Consistency and Consensus). Extract: the practitioner framing of Byzantine vs. crash faults and the total-order-broadcast/consensus equivalence. Applied in §1 Goal & Scope and §8 Domain Model.
  • Van Steen, M.; Tanenbaum, A. S. — Distributed Systems, 3rd ed., 2017 (distributed- systems.net). Ch. 8 (Fault Tolerance): Byzantine agreement and replicated state machines. Extract: the replicated-state-machine framing that unifies PBFT and HotStuff as instances of one abstraction. Applied in §29 Scalability Analysis and Comparative Architecture Table.

Papers

  • Lamport, L.; Shostak, R.; Pease, M., 1982 — The Byzantine Generals Problem. ACM TOPLAS 4(3). §3's 3f+1 lower-bound proof. Applied in §7 (I2) and Phase 12 (boot-time check).
  • Fischer, M.; Lynch, N.; Paterson, M., 1985 — Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process. JACM 32(2). FLP. Applied in §6 (P1) and Phase 15 (why liveness, not safety, is what a timer buys back).
  • Dwork, C.; Lynch, N.; Stockmeyer, L., 1988 — Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony. JACM 35(2). The eventual-synchrony model. Applied in Phase 15 (view-change timer) and §4 (RTT assumptions).
  • Bracha, G., 1987 — Asynchronous Byzantine Agreement Protocols. Information and Computation 75(2). The echo-broadcast ancestor of pre-prepare/prepare/commit. Applied in §8 Domain Model.
  • Castro, M.; Liskov, B., 1999 — Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance. OSDI 1999. The normative algorithm. Applied in Phase 13 (normal case) and Phase 15 (view change).
  • Castro, M., 2001 — Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance. MIT PhD Thesis, TR/MIT-LCS-TR-817. The long-form, thesis-normative version; fixes the OSDI paper's P-set low-water-mark ambiguity. Applied in Phase 15 (§15.6 pitfall) and Phase 17 (thesis section 2.3.6 state transfer).
  • Castro, M.; Liskov, B., 2002 — Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance and Proactive Recovery. ACM TOCS 20(4). Adds proactive recovery. Applied in the Deferred Features Register (§35).
  • Yin, M.; Malkhi, D.; Reiter, M.; Gueta, G.; Abraham, I., 2019 — HotStuff: BFT Consensus with Linearity and Responsiveness. PODC 2019. The linear pacemaker and three-chain commit rule. Applied in Phase 18.
  • Bano, S.; Sonnino, A.; Buchman, E.; Danezis, G., 2020 — Twins: BFT Systems Made Robust. arXiv:2004.10617. The adversarial-identity testing framework. Applied in Phase 21.
  • Buchman, E., 2016 — Tendermint: Byzantine Fault Tolerance in the Age of Blockchains. MSc Thesis, U. Guelph. The gossip-coupled, lock-based PBFT variant. Applied in the Comparative Architecture Table.
  • Chan, B. Y.; Pass, R.; Shi, E., 2020 — Streamlet: Textbook Streamlined Blockchains. AFT 2020. The two-phase, no-view-change BFT. Applied in Alternative Architectures.
  • Danezis, G.; Kogias, E.; Sonnino, A.; Spiegelman, A., 2022 — Narwhal and Tendermint: DAG-based Mempool and Efficient BFT Consensus. EuroSys 2022. The mempool/consensus decoupling. Applied in §29 Scalability Analysis and Alternative Architectures.

Reference Implementations

  • BFT-SMaRt (bft-smart.github.io). Study bft.consensus, bft.tom, bft.reconfiguration. Lesson: signed-vs-MAC and batching engineering trade-offs (Bessani-Sousa-Alchieri, DSN 2014). Pitfall not to copy: JVM GC pauses in the hot path. Applied in Phase 13, Phase 20.
  • CometBFT (github.com/cometbft/cometbft, formerly Tendermint Core). Study consensus, p2p, mempool. Lesson: gossip-coupled consensus. Pitfall not to copy: locking-rule complexity substituting for explicit view-change bookkeeping. Applied in Phase 15, Comparative Architecture Table.
  • libhotstuff (github.com/hot-stuff/libhotstuff). Study consensus.cpp, liveness.cpp. Lesson: the linear pacemaker in C++. Pitfall not to copy: manual memory management in the consensus hot path. Applied in Phase 18.
  • MIT PBFT reference library (Castro & Liskov's original C++ release, pmg.lcs.mit.edu/~castro/ pbft.html). Study the view-change and checkpoint modules. Lesson: the original P-set/O-set bookkeeping structures. Pitfall not to copy: unchecked buffer arithmetic in message parsing. Applied in Phase 15, Phase 16.
  • Diem/Aptos consensus (aptos-core consensus/ crate, DiemBFT lineage). Study the SafetyRules and RoundManager modules. Lesson: production HotStuff pacemaker and safety-rule separation. Pitfall not to copy: the safety-rules/consensus split adds an IPC boundary not needed at this project's scale. Applied in Phase 18.

Glossary (§36) · 47 terms · site-wide glossary

Authenticator
the pluggable trait (§11.3) abstracting MAC/Ed25519/BLS message authentication (ADR-05).
BLS12-381
a pairing-friendly elliptic curve used for threshold-signature aggregation (Phase 14 §14.2, Boneh-Lynn-Shacham 2001).
Byzantine fault
an arbitrary fault: incorrect, malicious, or adversarially coordinated behavior, not limited to crashing (Lamport-Shostak-Pease 1982).
Checkpoint
a periodic, quorum-certified snapshot of state-machine state used for GC and recovery (Phase 16, I7).
Client
the (untrusted) submitter of Request operations (§22 T6).
Committed-local
the predicate committed-local(m,v,n) (I4): prepared plus 2f+1 matching commits.
Common coin
a cryptographic primitive enabling randomized asynchronous agreement (Cachin-Kursawe-Shoup 2000); underlies the deferred HoneyBadger variant (§35).
Digest
a 32-byte BLAKE3 hash (§11.1, ADR-08) identifying a request batch or state root.
Eclipse attack
isolating a single replica from the rest of the cluster (§22 T5).
Epoch
the membership-reconfiguration unit; a new replica set takes effect at an epoch boundary (Phase 20, ADR-20).
Equivocation
a Byzantine primary or replica sending conflicting messages for the same (view, sequence) (§22 T1, T2; P10).
FLP impossibility
the Fischer-Lynch-Paterson 1985 result: no deterministic asynchronous consensus protocol is both safe and live with even one crash fault.
Garbage collection (GC)
pruning log entries below the stable checkpoint (Phase 16, I8).
High-water-mark
stable_seq + L; the upper bound on in-flight sequence numbers (I8, ADR-17).
HotStuff
the linear-view-change BFT protocol (Yin et al. 2019) implemented as the second core in Phase 18.
Liveness
the guarantee that the protocol eventually makes progress; secondary to safety (P1).
Low-water-mark
stable_seq; the lower bound below which log entries are prunable (I8).
MAC vector
a per-recipient keyed-hash authenticator; the OSDI-original, non-transferable authentication scheme (Phase 14 §14.2).
Merkleized snapshot
a state snapshot organized as a Merkle tree over fixed-size chunks, enabling differential state transfer (§11.6, ADR-19).
NewView
the message a new primary sends to complete a view change, embedding the O-set (§11.2, I6).
Non-determinism
any input to the state machine not derivable purely from (state, request); forbidden except as explicit request fields (P3, I12).
O-set
the set of re-proposed pre-prepares a new primary constructs during view change (Castro-Liskov section 4.4.2, I6).
Pacemaker
the pluggable view-advancement policy in bft-hotstuff (§11.5).
Partial synchrony
the Dwork-Lynch-Stockmeyer 1988 model: eventually, but not always, bounded message delay.
PBFT
Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (Castro-Liskov 1999); this project's primary consensus core.
Pipelining
allowing multiple sequence numbers to be in flight simultaneously, bounded by L (ADR-17).
PrePrepare
the primary's initial proposal message for a (view, sequence) (§11.2).
Prepared
the predicate prepared(m,v,n) (I3): matching pre-prepare plus 2f matching prepares.
Proof-of-possession (PoP)
a BLS key-registration technique defending against rogue-key attacks (§22 T9, I13).
Quorum
a subset of replicas whose agreement is sufficient for a protocol step; 2f+1 internally, f+1 for the client (I2, I9).
Quorum certificate (QC)
a HotStuff aggregate signature certifying 2f+1 votes for a proposal (§11.5, I13).
Read-only fast path
the 2f+1-quorum, single-round-trip path for non-mutating client operations (Phase 19 §19.2, I10).
Replica
one node in the n = 3f+1 cluster (§11.4).
Reliable broadcast
a broadcast primitive guaranteeing all correct processes deliver the same message set (Bracha 1987); conceptual ancestor of pre-prepare/prepare/commit.
Safety
the guarantee that no two correct replicas ever disagree; strictly dominant over liveness (P1, I1).
State machine replication (SMR)
the general technique of replicating a deterministic state machine across nodes to tolerate faults.
State transfer
the protocol recovering a lagging or crashed replica via merkleized snapshot exchange (Phase 17).
Stable checkpoint
a checkpoint certified by 2f+1 matching Checkpoint messages (I7).
Three-chain commit rule
HotStuff's commit condition: a block is committed once three consecutive quorum certificates chain from it (Phase 18 §18.2).
Threshold signature
a signature scheme where any t-of-n share subset can produce a valid aggregate (Boldyreva 2003; Phase 14 §14.2).
Twins
an adversarial testing framework simulating equivocation via duplicated replica identities (Bano et al. 2020; Phase 21).
View
a numbered epoch of the protocol with a designated primary; changes via view-change (§11.1).
View change
the protocol that elects a new primary and preserves safety across the transition (Phase 15).
View-change timer
the monotone, exponential timer triggering a view change on primary silence (I11, ADR-11).
WAL (write-ahead log)
the durable, append-only record of every signed outbound message, written before transmission (P13, Phase 16).
Wire format
the CBOR (RFC 8949) canonical encoding used for all consensus messages (ADR-04).
Zyzzyva
a speculative-execution BFT protocol (Kotla et al. 2007); compared in §31.
