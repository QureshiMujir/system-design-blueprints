In one paragraph

PBFT Byzantine Consensus is a design for a cluster of servers (replicas) that agree on one order for client operations even when up to f of them are Byzantine, meaning they may behave arbitrarily, maliciously or in coordination, not merely crash (§1, §36). It needs 3f+1 replicas (§1, I2) and assumes partial synchrony: message delay is eventually, but not always, bounded (§1, §36). The blueprint specifies a Rust stack: the Castro-Liskov core, a second HotStuff core, three interchangeable ways to authenticate messages, checkpointing, state transfer, membership reconfiguration and adversarial test harnesses (§1). A property it returns to throughout is that safety never depends on timing while progress does (§3, P1). It is for engineers building permissioned-consensus infrastructure such as validator clusters and ordering services, and for the on-call staff who run it (§1).

The problem, concretely

Picture a cluster that must apply the same operations in the same order on every replica. The blueprint names three things that go wrong at 3 a.m.: a primary that equivocates, meaning it sends conflicting pre-prepare messages; a replica that withholds its commits until the view-change timer expires; and a partition that splits the quorum into two halves of 2f (§2). A crash-only design such as Raft cannot cope. Its majority of f+1 out of 2f+1 has no defense against a lying replica, and it has no equivocation defense (§34 A4).

The equivocation case shows why. A Byzantine primary sends PrePrepare<v,n,d> to one part of the cluster and PrePrepare<v,n,d'> to another (§22 T1). The design answers with the PP1 check, which rejects the second message at any replica that sees both (§22 T1), and with quorums of 2f+1, because any two such quorums share at least one correct replica (§4, MM4). It also refuses to trade safety for availability: when safety is at risk the cluster stalls, and a stuck view change is a page, not a safety incident, until proven otherwise (P1). The price is three message rounds and quadratic message traffic in the number of replicas (§2, §4).

How it works, end to end

Follow one client request through the boxes of §3's diagram (normal-case part shown):

                            CLIENT

                              ▼
       ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
       │                  Primary (replica p)             │
       │       assigns sequence number n in view v        │
       └──────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┘

       ▼                      ▼                           ▼
   Backup r1              Backup r2                  Backup rN-1
       │  Prepare<v,n,d,i>_σi (i = own replica id)        │

              │   2f matching Prepare msgs    │ ──► prepared(m,v,n) TRUE (I3)

                              │ Commit<v, n, d, i>_σi   (multicast to all)
                              ▼
              ┌───────────────────────────────┐
              │   2f+1 matching Commit msgs   │ ──► committed-local(m,v,n) TRUE (I4)
              └───────────────────┬───────────┘
                                  │ Reply<v, t, c, i, r>_σi
                                  ▼
                        CLIENT collects f+1 matching replies (I9)
  1. Client (bft-cli/src/client.rs, §10). It sends a signed Request<o, t, c> with a monotonic timestamp t to the primary (§3). A replayed old request is caught by the per-client timestamp check and answered from a cached reply (T6). A flood is cut by per-client rate limiting at the bft-net edge (T10).
  2. Transport (bft-net). Authenticated TCP (transport.rs, ADR-15) carries length-prefixed CBOR frames (framing.rs, ADR-04). peer.rs dispatches signature checks to a bounded worker pool of min(cores,8) (ADR-18, §37). The Authenticator trait in bft-crypto supplies the MAC, Ed25519 or BLS backend (§11, ADR-05).
  3. Primary (bft-core, replica/normal.rs, §37). It assigns sequence number n in view v and multicasts PrePrepare<v, n, d> plus the batch (§3). A batch holds up to B=256 requests (ADR-16), and at most L=128 pre-prepares are in flight (ADR-17, §4); other sections give L=1024 (see "The numbers").
  4. Backups. The PP1 check rejects a pre-prepare whose embedded batch digest is not d, before it is logged, and no Prepare is sent. A pre-prepare with n ≤ stable_seq or n > high_water is discarded under I8's gate with no WAL write (§8.4). A good one makes the backup multicast Prepare (§8.2).
  5. Prepared. On 2f matching prepares from distinct non-primary replicas, the predicate prepared becomes true (I3, replica/predicates.rs, §37). The replica multicasts Commit (§3, §8.2).
  6. Committed-local. On 2f+1 matching commits the predicate committed-local becomes true (I4). The replica executes the request on the application state machine and sends Reply (§8.2). That state machine must be deterministic (I12).
  7. WAL. Every signed outbound message is written to the log in bft-storage (wal.rs) before it is sent, so a crashed replica can replay and resume safely (P13, §37).
  8. Client again. The client accepts a result only after f+1 matching replies from distinct replicas (I9, §3).

§3 draws three more boxes around this path: a view change when the primary goes silent (§3, §8.5), a checkpoint every K requests that lets old log entries be dropped (§3, I7, I8), and state transfer that rebuilds a lagging replica from a verified snapshot (§3). A second core, bft-hotstuff, makes the view change linear (§1, §29). The simulator/ harness runs scenarios with one assertion per invariant (§7).

The core ideas

Quorums: why 3f+1 and 2f+1 (§4, I2, MM4)

The cluster has n = 3f+1 replicas, so f = (n-1)/3 with integer division, and boot refuses a smaller n (§4, I2, P2). Every internal quorum (prepare, commit, checkpoint) is 2f+1, because any two such quorums intersect in at least one correct replica (§4). MM4 states the consequence: every other PBFT choice follows from that one arithmetic fact, and an internal threshold below 2f+1 is a bug (§30). The client is the exception: it needs only f+1 matching replies, because quorum intersection guarantees at least one correct reply among them (§4, I9). The read-only fast path uses 2f+1 instead, because it must intersect any concurrent write quorum (I10). The blueprint's own n=4 instance (§32 item 2, §8.3):

Quantity Formula Value at n=4
Faults tolerated f (n-1)/3 1
Quorum (prepare/commit/checkpoint) 2f+1 3
Prepares needed besides the pre-prepare 2f 2
Matching client replies f+1 2

As n grows the tolerated fraction f/n approaches one third and never exceeds it (§32 item 3).

The normal case as a state machine over predicates (§8.2, I3, I4, P4, MM2)

PBFT is specified in terms of two predicates over the log, not "messages I have received" (P4, MM2). prepared(m,v,n) holds when the log has a matching pre-prepare plus 2f matching prepares from distinct non-primary replicas (I3). committed-local(m,v,n) holds when prepared is true and there are 2f+1 matching commits (I4). The replica moves like this (§8.2):

State Event Guard Next Sends
Idle pre-prepare arrives (backup) PP1 passes PrePrepared Prepare
PrePrepared 2f distinct prepares prepared true (I3) Prepared Commit
Prepared 2f+1 distinct commits committed-local true (I4) Committed Reply; execute
any view-change timer fires I11 ViewChanging ViewChange

In the n=4 trace, r0 counts implicitly through its own pre-prepare, so r0 plus any 2f=2 backups make prepared true; 2f+1=3 commits make committed-local true; the client waits for f+1=2 replies (§8.3). Why predicates and not counters: tallying messages produces safety violations during view changes, because the P-set is the set of predicate-true bundles (§3, P4).

View change: handing safety to the next primary (§8.5, I5, I6, MM3)

When a primary goes silent, backups cannot just start over (MM3). The view-change message carries the P-set, the set of every (pre-prepare, 2f matching prepares) bundle above stable_seq for which prepared was ever true (I5). The new primary builds an O-set: for each sequence number, the digest from the highest-view P-set entry (I6). MM3 calls the P-set "a proof-carrying handoff, not a vote" (§30). The message shapes in §3:

   ┌─────────────────────────── VIEW CHANGE (Phase 15) ────────────────────────┐
   │  Timer expires → replica i sends ViewChange<v+1, n_low, C, P>_σi (I11)   │
   │  New primary p' collects 2f+1 ViewChange → builds NewView msg            │
   │  NewView<v+1, V, O> carries the O-set: re-emitted pre-prepares forced    │
   │  by the union of P-sets (I5, I6)                                        │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

The blueprint's trace (§8.5), with n=4:

  1. Primary r0 stops emitting pre-prepares. Backup timers fire at T_base=1s; each multicasts ViewChange<1, stable_seq, checkpoint_proof, prepared_set>.
  2. New primary r1 = 1 mod 4 (round-robin, ADR-12) collects 2f+1=3 matching ViewChanges, builds NewView<1, V, O> and multicasts it.
  3. Backups recompute O locally. On a match they re-enter PrePrepared for each re-proposed entry and continue the normal case; on a mismatch they reject and trigger the next view change (§8.2, I6).

If r0 had equivocated at seq=5 before going silent, the O-set entry at seq=5 is forced to the digest in the highest-view PreparedProof across V, which is what makes the change safe (§8.5). Under the MAC backend the selection rule is plurality rather than highest-view (I6). P6 sets the priority: correctness against I5 and I6 first, timer behavior second, the O(n²) to O(n) improvement (the HotStuff core) third (§6).

Checkpoints, garbage collection and state transfer (§3, I7, I8, MM8)

Every K requests each replica multicasts Checkpoint<n, d> (§3). K=128 (ADR-13). After 2f+1 matching checkpoints, n becomes the stable low-water mark (stable_seq); log entries below it may be pruned, and the high-water mark is stable_seq + L (§3, I7, I8, §36). stable_seq never moves backwards (I8). MM8 puts it plainly: garbage collection is a liveness optimization riding on a safety precondition, so a GC path that fires before the 2f+1 checkpoint is a latent safety bug (§30). A replica that falls behind asks f+1 replicas for state; the snapshot is a Merkle tree over 256-byte chunks (ADR-19) and is checked against the 2f+1 checkpoint proof before it is applied (§3). A pre-prepare or new-view referring to a sequence above the high-water mark sends the replica into StateTransferring (§8.2).

Authentication is a plug; the safety argument is not (P5, ADR-05, MM5, I13)

Three backends sit behind one Authenticator trait (§1, P5). MAC vectors are fastest on a LAN fan-out but are not third-party-transferable. Ed25519 enables forwarded evidence. BLS aggregates collapse a quorum certificate to 96 bytes (§3). MM5 states that swapping backends changes performance and evidence transferability, never the safety argument (§30). The throughput targets differ by backend: at n=4, MAC at least 20,000 ops/s, Ed25519 at least 4,000, BLS at least 10,000 (M3, M2, M1, §28). BLS adds its own rule: an aggregate is valid only if it embeds at least 2f+1 distinct partial signatures whose keys passed proof-of-possession, which defeats the rogue-key attack (I13, T9).

The timer is a liveness knob only (P1, P11, I11, ADR-11, MM6)

Safety holds under arbitrary delay; progress needs an eventually synchronous network, and the view-change timer is the bridge (§3). The timer starts at 1 s, doubles on each consecutive failed view, resets only when committed-local advances, and reads a monotonic clock, never wall time (ADR-11, P11, I11). MM6 follows: changing T_base can fix a liveness problem but never a safety bug, and a team that "fixes" equivocation by tuning a timer has a bug elsewhere (§30).

The trade-offs — where engineers disagree

Authenticator: selectable MAC / Ed25519 / BLS (ADR-05)

Chosen: a selectable backend behind one trait, because the security trade-offs differ by deployment (P5, §9). Rejected: a single fixed scheme. When the rejected choice is right: a fixed Ed25519-only scheme, in a small static-membership cluster where operational simplicity outweighs LAN throughput or quorum-certificate compaction (§9).

Garbage-collection gate: 2f+1 matching checkpoints (ADR-14)

Chosen: 2f+1 matching checkpoints, because 2f+1 intersects any prepare quorum and so guarantees GC safety (§9, I7). Rejected: f+1 matching checkpoints. When the rejected choice is right: f+1 is correct in a crash-fault-tolerant-only deployment (Raft or Paxos), where all replicas are trusted not to lie (§9).

View-change timer: monotone, exponential, base 1 s (ADR-11)

Chosen: a monotone exponential timer with base 1 s and factor 2, for NTP-attack resistance and backoff under repeated failure (§9, P11). Rejected: a fixed timer, or linear backoff. When the rejected choice is right: a fixed timer, in a tightly controlled LAN with a known worst-case round trip, where backoff only adds recovery latency after one transient blip (§9).

Checkpoint frequency: every 128 requests (ADR-13)

Chosen: every 128 requests, balancing WAL size against checkpoint bandwidth (§9). Rejected: every request, or every 1024. When the rejected choice is right: every-request checkpointing, for low-throughput, high-value ledgers such as interbank settlement, to minimize replay risk (§9). The blueprint is not consistent about how K relates to L: ADR-17 sets the pipeline depth to L=128 (§9), while §16.6 says K must satisfy K ≪ L and that "K=128, L=1024" were chosen for exactly that margin, and §33 item 5 asks the reader to derive why K ≪ L is required (§16.6, §33).

Membership change: epoch-based reconfiguration (ADR-20)

Chosen: a new replica set takes effect at an epoch boundary, the simplest protocol that provably preserves I14 (§9, §36). Rejected: live or instant reconfiguration. When the rejected choice is right: a large, high-churn validator set where the epoch-boundary delay is materially costly (§9, §35).

Roads not taken (§34, §35)

Leaderless DAG-BFT (§34 A1): workers broadcast certified batches in parallel and a separate protocol orders them (Narwhal and Bullshark, used by Sui). It wins in very high-throughput WAN deployments where dissemination, not agreement, is the bottleneck (§34). It is rejected because it adds a second protocol layer beyond the project's goal of teaching the Castro-Liskov core, and it is deferred as a stretch goal (§34, §35).

Nakamoto proof-of-work (§34 A2): the longest chain wins and finality is probabilistic. It wins in permissionless, open networks with no fixed validator set. It is rejected because probabilistic safety violates P9 and this design is permissioned, deterministic BFT: "a different problem, not a slower point on the same curve" (§34, P9).

Fully asynchronous BFT with a common coin (§34 A3): HoneyBadger BFT has no timing assumption and no view-change timer, and liveness is probabilistic while safety is not. It wins on adversarial WANs where an attacker controls scheduling indefinitely. It is rejected because it needs a different liveness argument from the partial-synchrony design, and it is deferred (§34, §35).

Crash-fault-tolerant consensus (§34 A4): Raft-style leader and majority acknowledgment. It wins in trusted-operator internal clusters where every node is assumed non-adversarial. It is rejected because it cannot survive arbitrary faults and has no analogue of P10's equivocation defense (§34, §1 non-goals).

Deferred register (§35): each parked feature has a trigger; sharded BFT, for example, comes back when the single-cluster throughput ceiling of §29 is reached in production (§35).

What must never be false

Each line gives the invariant, how the system notices, and the recovery where the blueprint gives one (§7).

  • I1: no two correct replicas commit different requests at the same (view, seq). Noticed by the Twins and Jepsen buckets; bft_safety_violation_total must stay 0; halt and dump a trace.
  • I2: n = 3f+1, quorum 2f+1. Startup validation; the replica refuses to start otherwise.
  • I3: prepared means a matching pre-prepare plus 2f matching prepares from distinct non-primary replicas. Conformance safety.predicate_p.
  • I4: committed-local means prepared plus 2f+1 matching commits. Conformance safety.predicate_c.
  • I5: the P-set holds every bundle above stable_seq for which prepared was ever true. Conformance view_change.p_set; bft_view_change_pset_size; re-derive locally and reject a malformed one.
  • I6: the O-set digest per sequence is the highest-view P-set entry (plurality under MAC). Conformance view_change.o_set; a mismatching NewView is rejected and the next view starts.
  • I7: a checkpoint is stable only after 2f+1 matching messages. Conformance checkpoint.gc_invariants; bft_stable_seq; a divergent replica is state-transferred.
  • I8: stable_seq never decreases; entries below it are pruned only after I7. bft_log_gc_bytes_freed; monotone by construction.
  • I9: the client accepts a result only after f+1 matching replies from distinct replicas. Conformance client.f_plus_one_matching; recovery is to retransmit to all replicas.
  • I10: the read-only fast path needs 2f+1 matching replies. Conformance client.read_only_fast_path; fallback is the full three-phase round.
  • I11: the view-change timer is monotonic-clock driven, exponential, reset only on committed-local advance. A lint forbids SystemTime; bft_view_change_timer_resets.
  • I12: (state, request) → (state', reply) is deterministic across correct replicas. Checkpoint cross-validation; bft_checkpoint_digest_mismatch_total; the divergent replica is state-transferred.
  • I13: a BLS aggregate needs at least 2f+1 distinct, proof-of-possession-verified partial signatures. Conformance crypto.bls_aggregate; a failure is treated as a forged certificate.
  • I14: a reconfiguration needs |R_old ∩ R_new| ≥ max(f_old,f_new)+1. Conformance membership.quorum_overlap; the request is rejected.

What breaks in production

  • FM1: primary equivocation. Symptom: two replicas hold different pre-prepare digests at one (v,n). Cause: a Byzantine primary (T1). Detection: a log conflict on insert (PP1). Recovery: the view change fires and the O-set heals it; the equivocation proof (both signed messages) is kept (§23).
  • FM2: view-change stall. Symptom: repeated view changes with no progress. Cause: Byzantine primary rotation or a persistent partition. Detection: bft_view_change_timer_resets grows without bound. Recovery: exponential backoff bounds the rate (ADR-11); escalate with the view-change-stuck.md runbook (§23, §10).
  • FM3: checkpoint divergence. Symptom: stable_seq frozen at one replica while peers advance. Cause: a non-deterministic state machine (an I12 violation) or a Byzantine replica. Detection: bft_checkpoint_digest_mismatch_total. Recovery: none automatic; state-transfer the divergent replica and compare Merkle roots (§23).
  • FM11: WAL corruption. Symptom: a replica fails to replay its WAL on restart. Cause: a crash during fsync. Detection: WAL checksum mismatch on replay. Recovery: replay up to the last valid checkpoint, then restore by state transfer; the corrupted segment is kept for audit (§23).
  • FM12: replay via a stale timestamp. Symptom: an old request is executed again. Cause: a missing last_timestamp[client] check (T6). Detection: the timestamp-monotonicity check. Recovery: the cached reply is returned without re-execution (§23).

Threats worth knowing (§22):

  • T1: a primary equivocating on pre-prepare. PP1 rejects the second message at any replica that sees both, and with a transferable authenticator the two signed messages are portable proof (§22, P10).
  • T9: a BLS rogue-key attack using a forged public key. Mitigated by proof-of-possession at key registration (§22, I13).
  • T11: an NTP attack on a wall-clock timer. Mitigated by using the monotonic clock only, enforced by a lint (§22, I11, P11).
  • T12: cross-view equivocation, where a replica prepares d in view v and d' in v+1. The O-set forces the highest-view prepared digest forward (§22, I5, I6).

How it compares

  • Tendermint/CometBFT merges the gossip layer with consensus and replaces the explicit view-change message with a locking rule tied to block height. That suits public or semi-public validator sets where one gossip substrate simplifies operations, at the cost of the P-set/O-set audit trail this design uses for forensics (§31).
  • HotStuff (DiemBFT/Aptos) swaps the quadratic view change for a linear pacemaker and a chain of quorum certificates, so each block carries its predecessor's justification forward. That wins at validator sets of 100 or more, where O(n²) view-change cost is the dominant liveness risk (§31, §29).
  • Zyzzyva adds a speculative fast path: replicas execute on one pre-prepare and reply at once, and fall back to a PBFT-style round only when the client sees disagreement. That is fine where adversaries are rare and the fallback is seldom paid (§31); this design does not take it as the default (§33 item 16).
  • SBFT uses linear, collector-based communication, optional threshold signatures and sharded execution, which separates agreement from execution throughput. That wins when execution, not agreement, is the bottleneck, a regime this design does not optimize for (§31).

Questions to test yourself

  1. Why does prepared(m,v,n) require 2f matching prepares excluding the primary's own, and what breaks if the primary's implicit prepare is double-counted? — Hint: I3 (§7) and §13.6.
  2. Trace what happens if a view change's O-set construction used the lowest-view P-set entry instead of the highest-view one — which invariant breaks and why? — Hint: §15.2 and I6 (§7).
  3. Derive why K ≪ L is required for the checkpoint/pipeline relationship to avoid stalling — what happens at the boundary K = L? — Hint: §16.6 and the §4 constraints table.
  4. Why is BLS proof-of-possession mandatory rather than optional hardening, and what specific attack does omitting it enable? — Hint: §14.6, T9 (§22) and I13 (§7).
  5. Why must the view-change timer read a monotonic clock instead of wall-clock time, and what concrete attack does a wall-clock timer enable? — Hint: P11 (§6) and T11 (§22).

If you only remember three things

Every quorum is 2f+1 because any two of them share a correct replica, and the one exception is the client's f+1 (MM4, I9). prepared and committed-local are predicates over the log, and the view-change P-set is a handoff of predicate-true bundles, not a vote (MM2, MM3). Safety never depends on timing and the timer is only a liveness knob, so a stuck view change is a page, not a safety incident (P1, MM6).

Words used here

  • Replica / primary / backup: one server in the cluster; the one proposing the order in the current view; the others.
  • View: a numbered epoch of the protocol with a designated primary (§36).
  • Quorum: a subset of replicas whose agreement suffices for a step; 2f+1 inside the cluster, f+1 for the client.
  • Prepared / committed-local: the two predicates over the log that gate sending Commit and executing.
  • P-set / O-set: the prepared bundles a replica reports, and the pre-prepares the new primary re-issues from them.
  • Checkpoint / stable checkpoint: a periodic, quorum-certified snapshot of state-machine state used for GC and recovery; one certified by 2f+1 matching Checkpoint messages (§36).
  • Transferable authenticator: one whose signed messages can be forwarded as evidence, unlike a MAC vector (§3, P10).
