Hard questions (§34)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. Why can the Master serve metadata in single-digit milliseconds while chunkservers move hundreds of MB/s, and what breaks if data were routed through the Master? (§3, §4, P1)
  2. Why is a 60-second lease sufficient to coordinate concurrent writers without a consensus round trip, and what fails if the lease never expired? (§8.3, §16)
  3. Trace, invariant by invariant, what happens when a primary dies between applying a write locally and acknowledging the client. (§8.7, §16.9)
  4. Why is record-append at-least-once rather than exactly-once, and what would exactly-once cost the Master? (§8.5, §17, ADR-21)
  5. Why must the placement function be re-evaluated at repair time rather than reusing the original allocation, and what invariant does skipping this violate? (§18.6, §21.1)
  6. What mechanism, specifically, keeps the failure detector from false-positiving a chunkserver dead under ordinary network jitter? (§4, §18.6)
  7. Why doesn't static federation (Phase 9) solve cross-subtree renames, and what would be required to support them? (§21.6)
  8. Why is Raft's read-index round trip necessary for a correct read, and what bug does skipping it reintroduce? (§22.2, §22.6)
  9. Derive the RAM ceiling on namespace size from the constraints table, and explain what breaks first as that ceiling is approached. (§4, §33 C1)
  10. Why does this design keep chunk data entirely out of the Raft log, and what happens to write throughput if it didn't? (P9, §22.7)
  11. Compare this design's placement mechanism to CRUSH's, and state the approximate cluster size at which the tradeoff flips. (§9 ADR-02/ADR-15, §32, §35)
  12. Why must crc.bin and data.bin be fsynced in the same durability window, and what does violating this actually corrupt? (§15.6, I4)
  13. Why is a torn WAL entry safe to discard on replay, but a torn checkpoint write is not — what makes the two cases different? (§14.8, §14.9)
  14. Why does snapshot deletion use reference counting rather than deep-copying chunk data, and what invariant breaks if refcounts go negative? (§20.2, I14)
  15. What is the exact chain of events that prevents split-brain during a Raft leader failover, and which single skipped step reintroduces it? (§22.2, §22.9)
  16. At what chunkserver count does the math in §33 actually justify switching from heartbeat-only membership to gossip? (§9 ADR-08, §33 C2)
  17. Why is scrubbing (Phase 7) necessary even though every read already verifies CRC (I4)? What corruption class does read-time verification alone miss? (§19.1, §19.9)
  18. Why does this design reject running data through consensus even at Phase 10, when Raft is already available? (P9, §22.2, §35)
  19. What three §4 numbers combine to produce the re-replication-time estimate in §33, and what operational lever shrinks it? (§4, §33 C7, §18.6)
  20. Why is the mount table (Phase 9) described as "the new coordination point," and what does that imply about federation versus full disaggregation (Colossus/Tectonic)? (§21.6, MM7, §32)
  21. Why does the design accept that a chunkserver can hold two on-disk copies of the same logical record-append entry (§8.8's worked example), and why does that not violate any System Invariant? (§8.8, I9)
  22. Why is scrubbing's minimum sustained throughput (§33 C8) so small relative to the read-bandwidth ceiling (§33 C5), and what does that imply about how much scrubbing "costs" a live cluster? (§33 C5, §33 C8, §19.4)

31. Mental Models & First Principles

  • MM1 — The Master is a cache of ground truth, not the truth itself.
    • Insight: the operation log is ground truth; in-RAM state is a materialized cache rebuilt by replay.
    • Predicts: any in-RAM-only optimization needs a WAL-replay equivalent.
    • Bug class without it: a field added to Inode in RAM but never logged vanishes on restart.
    • Applies at: §14 (Phase 2), I8.
  • MM2 — A lease is a clock, not a lock.
    • Insight: unlike a mutex, a lease's validity is time-bounded and must be renewed.
    • Predicts: every write path must check epoch/expiry, not just "did I get the lease once."
    • Bug class: a writer keeps writing after silent lease expiry, producing a split-brain-like double-writer scenario.
    • Applies at: §16 (Phase 4), I3, I10.
  • MM3 — The chunk is the file system's transaction boundary.
    • Insight: replication/repair/checksums are chunk-granular because that unit is small enough to move over a LAN quickly and large enough to amortize metadata overhead.
    • Predicts: any sub-chunk-atomicity feature fights the architecture.
    • Bug class: reinventing whole-chunk transfer badly by trying to repair "just the changed bytes" without the Merkle structure.
    • Applies at: §8.2, §19 (Phase 7).
  • MM4 — At-least-once is a promise about the log, not about your application.
    • Insight: record-append guarantees a record lands somewhere, once, per replica — not that your reader processes it once.
    • Predicts: any consumer without a dedup key double-processes under retry.
    • Bug class: double-counted downstream aggregates fed by an append log.
    • Applies at: §17 (Phase 5), P5, I9.
  • MM5 — Every failure detector is a bet between speed and false positives.
    • Insight: heartbeat interval and dead-threshold are a tuned tradeoff, not free parameters.
    • Predicts: tightening detection without handling flapping causes repair storms.
    • Bug class: an aggressive-detection "fix" that triggers ten spurious re-replication waves.
    • Applies at: §18 (Phase 6), §4.
  • MM6 — Determinism is what makes crash recovery a checkable property, not an act of faith.
    • Insight: WAL replay and placement are deterministic by design so recovery is testable (replay-twice-and-diff), not merely hoped for.
    • Predicts: any hidden nondeterministic step (wall-clock read, unordered-map iteration) breaks the replay-equality test.
    • Bug class: "recovery worked in staging, diverged in prod."
    • Applies at: I7, I8, §21.1.
  • MM7 — Federation trades one hot spot for a routing problem.
    • Insight: splitting the Master relocates coordination to whatever tells clients which Master to use.
    • Predicts: the mount table becomes the next thing that must stay available and consistent.
    • Bug class: assuming federation removed the single point of coordination instead of shrinking it.
    • Applies at: §21 (Phase 9).
  • MM8 — Consensus buys you exactly one property: everyone agrees on one order.
    • Insight: Raft exists solely to produce an agreed-upon operation sequence — it says nothing about data-plane latency/throughput, which is why data never runs through it here.
    • Predicts: routing bulk data through the consensus log collapses throughput to metadata-op speed.
    • Bug class: "replicate everything through Raft for simplicity" designs that can't explain their own throughput ceiling.
    • Applies at: P9, §22 (Phase 10).

Required reading (§5)

Books

  • Kleppmann, Designing Data-Intensive Applications (O'Reilly, 2017), ch. 5 (Replication) + ch. 9 (Consistency and Consensus) — the consistency-model taxonomy used to classify the GFS-weak default. Applied in §8.5, §30.
  • Petrov, Database Internals (O'Reilly, 2019), Part II — log-structured storage and replicated-state-machine patterns behind the Master's WAL. Applied in §14 (Phase 2).
  • Tanenbaum & Van Steen, Distributed Systems (3rd ed., 2017), ch. 7 (Consistency and Replication) + ch. 8 (Fault Tolerance) — the failure-detector taxonomy (crash-stop vs. crash-recovery) used for heartbeat design. Applied in §18 (Phase 6), §24.
  • Arpaci-Dusseau & Arpaci-Dusseau, Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (2018), file-system and RAID chapters — on-disk block layout and checksum placement, reused for the chunk's crc.bin sidecar. Applied in §15 (Phase 3).
  • Cachin, Guerraoui, Rodrigues, Introduction to Reliable and Distributed Programming (2nd ed., Springer, 2011) — formal definitions of reliable broadcast and consensus, used to justify why the write pipeline needs no full consensus. Applied in §8.4, §16 (Phase 4).
  • Beyer et al. (Google), Site Reliability Engineering (O'Reilly, 2016), ch. 22 (Addressing Cascading Failures) + ch. 26 (Data Processing Pipelines) — the operational discipline behind Operational Readiness. Applied in §25, §28.
  • McKusick, Neville-Neil, Watson, The Design and Implementation of the FreeBSD Operating System (2nd ed., 2014), local FFS chapter — fixed-block allocation and superblock-checkpoint patterns mirrored in the chunk store's meta.bin. Applied in §15 (Phase 3).

Papers

  • Ghemawat, Gobioff, Leung. The Google File System. SOSP 2003 — the architecture this project descends from. Applied in §3, §8, §16 (Phase 4).
  • Shvachko, Kuang, Radia, Chansler. The Hadoop Distributed File System. MSST 2010 — fifteen years of the same design under production load. Applied in §18 (Phase 6), §25.
  • Weil, Brandt, Miller, Long, Maltzahn. Ceph: A Scalable, High-Performance Distributed File System. OSDI 2006 — the alternative to a centralized Master: dynamic subtree partitioning. Applied in §21 (Phase 9), §32.
  • Weil, Brandt, Miller, Maltzahn. CRUSH: Controlled, Scalable, Decentralized Placement of Replicated Data. SC 2006 — deterministic, table-free placement; the deferred alternative. Applied in §35, §36.
  • Thekkath, Mann, Lee. Frangipani: A Scalable Distributed File System (SOSP 1997); Lee & Thekkath, Petal: Distributed Virtual Disks (ASPLOS 1996) — a layered design separating concerns the way this project separates Master and chunkserver. Applied in §8.3, §35.
  • Pan et al. (Meta). Facebook's Tectonic Filesystem: Efficiency from Exascale. FAST 2021 — disaggregated metadata at exabyte scale, the production answer to the single-Master bottleneck. Applied in §21 (Phase 9), §32.
  • Ongaro & Ousterhout. In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm. USENIX ATC 2014 — the Raft protocol and read-index construction used for Master HA. Applied in §22 (Phase 10).
  • Lamport. The Part-Time Parliament. ACM TOCS 1998 — the original Paxos paper, the consensus alternative evaluated and rejected for Master HA. Applied in §9 (ADR-19), §35.
  • Google Cloud. Colossus: Successor to the Google File System (engineering design notes, Google, various dates) — the production evolution of GFS toward disaggregated, multi-tenant metadata. Applied in §21 (Phase 9), §32.

Reference Implementations

  • Apache Hadoop HDFS (github.com/apache/hadoop), hadoop-hdfs-project/.../server/namenode/{FSNamesystem,BlockManager}.java — canonical reference for namespace-tree operations and the under-replicated-blocks queue. Applied in §13 (Phase 1), §18 (Phase 6).
  • Ceph (github.com/ceph/ceph), src/mds/, src/crush/ — dynamic subtree partitioning and the CRUSH engine. Applied in §21 (Phase 9), §35.
  • SeaweedFS (github.com/seaweedfs/seaweedfs), weed/storage/, weed/server/ — a small-object-biased chunkserver design, contrast with fixed 64 MiB chunks. Applied in §15 (Phase 3), §32.
  • JuiceFS (github.com/juicedata/juicefs), pkg/meta/, pkg/chunk/ — metadata in a pluggable KV store, data in object storage. Applied in §21 (Phase 9), §32.
  • MooseFS (github.com/moosefs/moosefs), mfsmaster/, mfschunkserver/ — a goal-driven chunk-loop scheduler close in spirit to this project's re-replication worker. Applied in §18 (Phase 6).
  • GlusterFS (github.com/gluster/glusterfs), xlators/cluster/dht/ — distributed hash table placement with no central metadata server. Applied in §35.

RFCs / Specifications

  • IEEE Std 1003.1 (POSIX.1), file-system interface clauses — the semantics deliberately not fully implemented. Applied in §1, §36.
  • RFC 3720 (iSCSI), Appendix B — the CRC32C polynomial and reference used for per-block chunk checksums. Applied in §15 (Phase 3).
  • gRPC / Protocol Buffers Language Guide (proto3) — wire-schema conventions for every Master/chunkserver/client RPC. Applied in §10, §11.

Glossary (§37) · 52 terms · site-wide glossary

Master
the metadata server: namespace tree, chunk map, replica map, operation log, checkpoints; called NameNode in HDFS, MDS in Ceph.
Chunkserver
a host storing fixed-size chunks and serving direct read/write/append RPCs.
Chunk
the fixed-size (64/128 MiB) atomic unit of storage, replication, and repair.
Block
the 64 KiB checksum granularity within a chunk.
ChunkId
the 64-bit, never-reused cluster-scope chunk identifier.
FileId
the 64-bit, never-reused namespace-scope inode identifier.
Inode
the per-file/directory metadata record (mode, owner, timestamps, chunk list).
Namespace tree
the hierarchical FileId-keyed structure of directories and files.
Operation log (WAL)
the append-only, fsync-per-entry durability log every Master mutation passes through before being applied.
Checkpoint
a periodic full serialization of Master state used to bound WAL replay time.
Lease
a time-bounded grant of write authority for one chunk to one chunkserver (the primary).
Lease epoch
a monotonically increasing counter per chunk rejecting stale-authority writes.
Primary replica
the chunkserver currently holding a chunk's write lease.
Secondary replica
a non-primary member of a chunk's replica set.
ReplicaSet
the ordered set of chunkservers holding copies of a chunk.
Replication factor
the configured number of replicas per chunk (default 3).
Placement function
the deterministic function mapping (chunk_id, topology, durability) to a replica set.
Failure domain
a rack/zone/region boundary replicas must be spread across (I2).
Heartbeat
the periodic (3 s) chunkserver→Master liveness + chunk-report message.
Suspect state
a chunkserver status after 3 missed heartbeats.
Dead state
a chunkserver status after 10 missed heartbeats, triggering re-replication.
Under-replicated queue
the Master's priority queue of chunks below target replication factor.
Re-replication
the background process restoring a chunk's replica count.
Record-append
the atomic, at-least-once, one-chunk-bounded shared-log write RPC.
GFS-weak consistency
this design's default: last-writer-wins within a lease epoch, at-least-once append.
CRC32C
the per-block checksum algorithm (RFC 3720) detecting corruption.
Merkle tree
the per-chunk hash tree over 1 MiB pages enabling bandwidth-bounded anti-entropy diffs.
Anti-entropy
background reconciliation of divergent replicas via Merkle-tree comparison.
Scrubbing
the periodic full-cluster read-and-verify background job (§19).
Snapshot
a point-in-time, copy-on-write capture of a namespace subtree.
Copy-on-write
sharing underlying chunk bytes across a live subtree and its snapshot until one diverges.
Reference counting
the mechanism preventing chunk deletion while a snapshot still references it (I14).
Quota
a configured byte/file-count cap on a namespace subtree.
Federation
static, per-top-level-directory partitioning of the namespace across multiple Masters (Phase 9).
Mount table
the client-side map from top-level directory to owning federated Master.
Raft
the leader-election + log-replication consensus protocol used for Master HA (Phase 10).
Read-index
Raft's protocol for confirming leadership before serving a linearizable read.
Split-brain
two processes simultaneously believing they hold write authority for the same state (FM-1).
Fencing
forcibly preventing a deposed leader from acting before a new leader proceeds.
RTO
Recovery Time Objective, the target maximum outage duration.
RPO
Recovery Point Objective, the target maximum acceptable data/operation loss window.
Durability mode
the per-subtree choice between replication and (deferred) erasure coding.
Erasure coding
a parity-based durability scheme trading storage overhead for repair bandwidth (deferred, §36).
CRUSH
Ceph's deterministic, tableless placement algorithm (deferred alternative, §35).
FUSE
the Linux userspace file-system protocol (deferred client surface, §36).
Client chunk cache
the client-side cache of a file's chunk-list/replica-list, TTL-bounded.
Stale epoch
a rejected write whose lease epoch is lower than the chunk's current epoch (I10).
Pipeline write
the chained primary→secondary→secondary write-forwarding protocol (§8.4).
Serial number
the primary-assigned, per-chunk-monotonic ordering token for pipeline writes.
Conformance fixture
a named, versioned test case with expected input/output driving the harness (§27).
Chaos test
a fault-injection scenario asserting invariant recovery within an SLA.
Deterministic simulator
the single-process fault-injection harness used for invariant/replay verification (§7 methodology).
