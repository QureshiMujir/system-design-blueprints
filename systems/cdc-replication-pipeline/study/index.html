Hard questions (§35)

Self-examination questions from the blueprint; each points at the section where the answer lives.

  1. Why is "exactly-once delivery" an incorrect claim for this system, and what does the pipeline actually guarantee instead? (§8, §16)
  2. Walk through a crash immediately after step 4 (idempotent upsert) but before step 5 (recording the dedup key) in the exactly-once-effect sequence table — why is the net effect still correct? (§8, §16)
  3. Why does the slot advance use min() across all sinks rather than any single sink's committed offset? What breaks with max() instead? (§8, I6)
  4. Why is primary-key-range chunking preferred over LIMIT/OFFSET paging for the snapshot reader, and what concretely goes wrong with OFFSET under concurrent writes? (§17, §5)
  5. In the DBLog watermark handoff, why must a window event always win over the corresponding snapshot image, never the reverse? Construct the resulting failure if reversed. (§8, §18)
  6. Why is DDL modeled as a first-class ordered stream event rather than an out-of-band side channel, and what incident class does this prevent? (§8, §19, FM-7)
  7. The system relaxes global LSN order to per-key order for sink-apply parallelism. Why is this relaxation safe, and what invariant breaks if relaxed further to no ordering guarantee at all? (I4, §31)
  8. Why does a replication slot represent a source-side risk rather than a pipeline-side inconvenience, and what two-threshold mechanism contains that risk? (I10, T2, §22)
  9. Compare the crash-window analysis at step 4 vs step 6 of the exactly-once-effect sequence table — why is a crash at step 6 recoverable without data loss, but a naive implementation without idempotent apply would not be? (§8, §16)
  10. Why does schema-compatibility enforcement happen at the registry boundary rather than at each sink, and what breaks with a per-sink-only design? (ADR-10, §19)
  11. Why are transform-engine failures DLQ'd rather than retried indefinitely, and how does this connect to the poison-event denial-of-service threat? (§8, §21, T6)
  12. Why is the coordination-store QPS bound a function of fleet size and lease-renewal frequency, and what two independent levers keep it under budget as the fleet grows? (§22, §31, §34)
  13. If a table's handoff is interrupted by a Truncate, why must the handoff abort and restart rather than reconcile the truncate into the window buffer? (§8, §18.8)
  14. Why does this design reject two-phase commit as the default exactly-once mechanism, and under what sink condition would 2PC actually be correct? (ADR-08)
  15. Why is a fleet-wide average lag metric rejected as a dashboard design in favor of per-source/per-sink breakdowns, and what failure would an average mask? (§20.10, §26)
  16. Why is commit_ts excluded from the ordering model despite being in every envelope, and what bug appears if a future engineer "optimizes" ordering to use it? (§8, §11, ADR-24, MM3)
  17. Why doesn't bounding retries at the individual-event level (ADR-16), by itself, protect against a systemic poison-event storm? (§21, §25 FM-10)
  18. Why is structural completeness necessary but not sufficient for correctness confidence in this system, and what additional check closes the gap? (§23, §28)
  19. Contrast the single-writer source-log throughput ceiling with the per-connector throughput target — why does §34's arithmetic show the pipeline itself, not the source, as the binding constraint at typical hardware, and under what condition does that flip? (§31, §34)
  20. Why is a rolling upgrade safe within one major version but not guaranteed safe across a major version, in terms of which compatibility properties hold in each case? (§27, P14)

32. Mental Models & First Principles

MM1 — CDC is the database's write-ahead log escaping the database. Everything downstream is a materialized view over that log (Kreps). Predicts: every sink is a different materialization strategy of the same log, not a bespoke integration. Bug class without it: treating each Sink as a one-off instead of a uniform trait over one canonical stream. Applies in §8, §11.

MM2 — Exactly-once delivery is a myth; exactly-once effect is achievable. The trick is always idempotence plus atomic offset commit, never a cleverer transport (Helland). Predicts: any "exactly-once" claim not backed by an idempotency key and an atomic commit boundary is wrong. Bug class without it: chasing transport-level guarantees that don't exist instead of building idempotent apply. Applies in §8, §16, P2.

MM3 — The LSN is the only clock that matters. Wall-clock timestamps are for humans, not ordering (Lamport). Predicts: a bug blamed on host clock skew is a red herring if the pipeline correctly orders by LSN, not commit_ts. Bug class without it: subtle reordering under multi-host clock skew. Applies in §8, §11, ADR-24.

MM4 — The snapshot-to-stream handoff is the one place a replica can silently diverge. It deserves more test effort than the whole streaming path (DBLog). Predicts: an under-tested handoff is the most likely source of "my new sink doesn't match the source." Bug class without it: a naive lock-based or fixed-cutover snapshot silently drops/duplicates rows under concurrent writes. Applies in §8, §17, §18.

MM5 — A replication slot is a liability the source carries on the pipeline's behalf. Every second of pipeline lag is a second of the source's disk at risk (a DBLog/Databus operational lesson). Predicts: watching only pipeline-side lag metrics leaves an operator blindsided by a source-side outage. Bug class without it: alerting on end-to-end lag alone and missing the slot-retention risk until the source pages first. Applies in §4, I10, §26.

MM6 — Schema is just another stream. DDL is data about data and must be ordered exactly like every other event (Debezium's envelope design). Predicts: any system treating DDL as an out-of-band side channel will eventually apply DML against the wrong schema version. Bug class without it: a race between a schema migration and in-flight DML. Applies in §8, §19.

MM7 — Per-key order is the invariant; global order is a convenience. Relax the convenience for throughput, never the invariant (Bernstein & Goodman). Predicts: naive "just parallelize by shard" designs that drop per-key order will corrupt data under concurrent updates to the same row. Bug class without it: a delete-then-insert on the same key applying out of order under parallel apply. Applies in §8, §20, §31.

MM8 — Backpressure is a contract, not an accident. An unbounded queue anywhere is a promise the system cannot keep (a queueing-theory first principle). Predicts: any component without an explicit bound eventually becomes the one that exhausts memory under a slow downstream. Bug class without it: unbounded growth cascading into a replication-slot bloat incident. Applies in §21, I13, P6.

Reference Implementations

  • Debezium — debezium-connector-postgres/, debezium-core/ — lesson: decouples decode from the Kafka Connect framework; pitfall: don't couple decode logic to the Connect API the way early Debezium did, which makes standalone reuse hard — applied in Phase 2 (§14), ADR-14.
  • Maxwell — MySQL binlog daemon — lesson: minimal-footprint row-based decode; pitfall: weaker schema-evolution story than Debezium — applied in Phase 2 (§14), §33.
  • PostgreSQL pgoutput/wal2json/test_decoding plugins — lesson: pgoutput is binary and low-overhead, wal2json is JSON and debuggable; pitfall: test_decoding is not production-safe — applied in Phase 1 (§13), ADR-04.
  • Kafka Connect — offset-management model (per-partition offset storage, rebalance protocol) — lesson: the source-partition offset abstraction generalizes cleanly; pitfall: its exactly-once semantics still require the sink connector's own idempotence — applied in Phase 4 (§16), Phase 10 (§22), ADR-08.
  • Netflix DBLog — the watermark-based snapshot algorithm — lesson: chunked, lock-free snapshot interleaved with the stream, dedup by watermark window; pitfall: naive implementations reorder rows crossing a chunk boundary — applied in Phase 6 (§17), §8.
  • Airbyte — connector-catalog/normalization approach — lesson: declarative catalog/stream discovery; pitfall: at-least-once-only delivery in community connectors — applied in §33, ADR-08.
  • Materialize — source connectors (Postgres/Kafka ingestion) — lesson: treats CDC input as a consistent-snapshot dataflow source; pitfall: tightly coupled to its own differential-dataflow engine — applied in §33, §36.

Glossary (§38) · 47 terms · site-wide glossary

Change Data Capture (CDC)
the practice of identifying and capturing row-level changes made to a database so they can be propagated elsewhere. 2.
Write-Ahead Log (WAL)
the durable, append-only log a database writes before applying a change; the authoritative record this pipeline tails. 3.
Logical Decoding
PostgreSQL's mechanism for interpreting WAL bytes as a stream of logical row-level changes, rather than physical page changes. 4.
Replication Slot
a PostgreSQL server-side object retaining WAL until a consumer confirms processing it; the mechanism behind the slot-retention risk (I10). 5.
LSN (Log Sequence Number)
the monotonic position of a change within the source's log; this system's total-order key (P1). 6.
Binlog
MySQL's write-ahead-log equivalent, read in row-based format for CDC. 7.
Oplog
MongoDB's operation log; the log-based capture source for a MongoDB connector (deferred, §37). 8.
Change Event
the canonical unit this pipeline emits per source mutation (§8). 9.
Before Image
a row's state prior to a mutation, carried in ChangeEvent.before. 10.
After Image
a row's state following a mutation, carried in ChangeEvent.after. 11.
Tombstone
a marker record representing a deletion in an append-only sink that cannot physically remove data (§20.8). 12.
Snapshot
a consistent, point-in-time read of a table's current rows, used to bootstrap a new sink (§17). 13.
Bootstrap
bringing a new sink from empty to caught-up, combining snapshot and handoff. 14.
Watermark
a point-in-time LSN marker injected into the stream to bound a snapshot chunk's reconciliation window (§8). 15.
Handoff
the coordinated transition from snapshot-sourced events to live-stream-sourced events for a table (§8, §18). 16.
Exactly-Once Effect
the property that every change is applied at most once in observable effect at a sink, even though the transport is only at-least-once (P2). 17.
Idempotent Apply
an apply operation producing the same end state no matter how many times it is repeated with the same input. 18.
Upsert
an insert-or-update operation keyed by a unique identifier; the mechanism behind idempotent apply at relational sinks. 19.
Offset
a position marker (here, an LSN) recording how far a sink has consumed the stream. 20.
Checkpoint
a durably committed offset; the unit of crash-resume state (§8, §16). 21.
At-Least-Once
a delivery guarantee that a message may be delivered more than once but never zero times. 22.
At-Most-Once
a delivery guarantee that a message may be lost but is never duplicated. 23.
Dead-Letter Queue (DLQ)
a durable holding area for events that cannot be applied, isolating them from the healthy stream (§8, §21). 24.
Poison Event
an event that repeatedly fails to apply, whose unbounded retry would starve the pipeline. 25.
Backpressure
a flow-control signal throttling upstream production when downstream consumption falls behind (§21). 26.
Schema Evolution
a source table's structure changing over time (add/drop/rename/type-change) and that change propagating downstream. 27.
Schema Registry
the durable, versioned store of table schemas used to gate and translate DDL (§8, §19). 28.
Compatibility Mode
a policy (BACKWARD/FORWARD/FULL/NONE) governing which schema changes are considered safe (§27). 29.
DDL Propagation
the mechanism by which a source's data-definition-language statement reaches and is applied at every sink (§8, §19). 30.
Replication Lag
the delay between a change committing at the source and its effect becoming visible at a sink. 31.
Connector
the process instance running capture, decode, snapshot, handoff, and dispatch for one source (§1). 32.
Rebalance
the redistribution of table/source ownership across a connector fleet when membership changes (§22). 33.
Sink
a downstream system this pipeline applies changes to (database, search index, warehouse, log). 34.
Source
the upstream database this pipeline tails; never written to by this pipeline (§1). 35.
Total Order
a complete, transitive ordering of all events from a source, derived from LSN (§8). 36.
Per-Key Order
the guarantee that events for the same primary key apply in source order at a sink, even if global order is relaxed elsewhere (I4). 37.
Idempotency Key
a deterministic identifier derived from table, primary key, and LSN used to detect and drop duplicate effects (§11). 38.
Coordination Store
an external, consensus-backed store (etcd/Consul-class) used for slot-ownership leases and fleet state (§22). 39.
Lease
a time-bounded, renewable ownership claim over a slot or table enforcing single-writer semantics (P4). 40.
Split-Brain
a failure state where two nodes each believe they hold the same lease/ownership, risking dual-write (§22.8). 41.
Fleet
the set of connector processes coordinating across multiple sources/sinks (§1, §22). 42.
Watermark Window
the LSN interval between a chunk's low and high watermark, during which stream events for that chunk's keys are buffered for reconciliation (§8). 43.
Reconciliation
merging a snapshot's row image with any newer window event for the same key (§8). 44.
RPO (Recovery Point Objective)
the maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured here in events (§4). 45.
RTO (Recovery Time Objective)
the maximum acceptable time to restore service after a failure (§4). 46.
Correctness Harness
the Jepsen/Elle-style fault-injected verifier that proves a sink converges to the source (§23). 47.
Differential Testing
comparing a sink's state to the source's state after a workload to check convergence (§28). ---
